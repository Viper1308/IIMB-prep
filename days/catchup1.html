L10 · Circular and two-row arrangements

A circle has no ends, so every clue is about relative position. Fix one person, draw the slots, and place people by their distance from each other. Two-row arrangements are the same skill with a mirror trap. About 35 minutes including the drill.

1. What changes from a straight line

  • No ends and no first seat. Rotating everyone round the table gives the same arrangement, so n people can sit in (n − 1)! distinct ways. You fix one person at the top and measure everyone from them.
  • Left and right depend on the way people face. Facing the centre, a person's left is clockwise (as seen from above) and right is anticlockwise. Facing outward it reverses. Write "L = clockwise" on your diagram before you start.
  • Opposite exists only for an even number of seats: the person n/2 places away. In a circle of 8, opposite means 4 places away; with 7 there is no opposite.
  • "Between" is ambiguous: two people split the circle into two arcs. If the question says "exactly 2 persons sit between A and B", it may be true in both directions unless one direction is named. With n people, the two arcs hold k and n − 2 − k persons.
Distance d clockwise from X to Y: the other way round is n − d
People strictly between them: d − 1 one way, n − d − 1 the other
Opposite (n even): d = n/2

2. The method

  1. Draw a circle with n slots and decide the direction convention (L = clockwise for inward-facing).
  2. Anchor on the person who appears in the most clues, or on a fixed fact (opposite, neighbours of ...). Place them at the top.
  3. Place the definite clues first: immediate left or right, second to the left, opposite.
  4. Use positive clues before negative ones. "Not neighbours" only eliminates options and is applied last.
  5. If a clue gives two possibilities (for example "neighbour of X" without a side), branch: try each and kill one with a later clue. Do not guess.
  6. Before answering, re-read every clue against the finished circle.
Trap: flipping left and right. If the question says "second to the right of P", walk anticlockwise two seats (inward-facing). Check the direction word before writing the name.

3. Mixed facing: inward and outward

When some people face the centre and others face out, draw an arrow on each seat. For a person facing outward, left is anticlockwise. A typical clue "A faces the centre and B, immediately to A's left, faces outward" is fine: just place B one seat clockwise of A, then mark B's arrow. The second step, "who is to B's left?", now runs the opposite way round.

4. Two-row arrangements

Two rows face each other: row 1 faces south (its left is east), row 2 faces north (its left is west). When you draw both rows on paper with the same left-right order, the two rows have opposite definitions of left and right.

  • The person directly across is in the same column.
  • Diagonally opposite is one column over and in the other row.
  • Fix the direction for each row in a note: "R1: left = east. R2: left = west."
  • Check the end seats: the extreme left of row 1 is across from the extreme right of row 2, because they stand at the same (east) end of the room.

5. Worked examples

a) Eight people sit round a table facing the centre. A is opposite B. C is second to the left of A. D is immediately to the right of C. E is opposite D. Where does E sit relative to A?

Put A at position 0 and B at 4 (opposite). Left is clockwise, so C, second to the left of A, is at position 2. D is immediately to C's right, so one place anticlockwise: position 1. E is opposite D: position 5. From A, position 5 is three places anticlockwise, so E sits third to the right of A (and next to B).

b) Seven people sit round a table. Exactly two persons sit between P and Q going clockwise from P. How many sit between them going anticlockwise from P?

d = 3 clockwise, so the other way is n − d = 4 steps, i.e. 7 − 3 − 1 = 3 persons between. Check: 2 + 3 + P + Q = 7.

c) Two rows of 4 face each other. In row 1 (facing south) P sits at the left end. Who sits across from P in row 2 (facing north)?

Row 1 faces south, so its left end is the east end. The person across from P is at the east end of row 2, and for row 2 (facing north) east is its right end. So P is across from row 2's right-end person. Draw both rows with arrows to check.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. The set is the unit of work: spend 90 seconds on the arrangement, then 30 seconds per question.
  2. Anchor, place positives, branch only when forced. If two branches survive after all positive clues, apply the negative clues.
  3. If you are stuck at 90 seconds with no definite placement, skip the whole set.

L11 · Indices and logarithms (deep)

A logarithm is an exponent in disguise: loga b answers "a to what power gives b?". Every log law is an index law read backwards, so we derive the indices first and the logarithm laws fall out. About 80 minutes: read for 45, examples for 20, then Drill 11.

1. Index laws, and where they come from

an (n a positive integer) means n copies of a multiplied. Everything else is defined so that the first law keeps working.

a^m × a^n = a^(m+n) (m + n copies)
a^m ÷ a^n = a^(m−n)
(a^m)^n = a^(mn)
(ab)^n = a^n b^n, (a/b)^n = a^n/b^n

Zero and negative powers. a^m ÷ a^m = 1 and also a^(m−m) = a^0, so a^0 = 1 (a ≠ 0). Likewise a^0 ÷ a^n = a^(−n), so a^(−n) = 1/a^n. Fractional powers. (a^(1/n))^n = a^1, so a^(1/n) is the n-th root, and a^(m/n) = (a^(1/n))^m.

Traps: a^m + a^n has no simple law (only common factors: 2^5 + 2^3 = 2^3(4 + 1) = 40). (−2)^4 = 16 but −2^4 = −16. a^(b^c) means a^(b^c), not (a^b)^c: 2^(3^2) = 2^9 = 512, while (2^3)^2 = 64. √(a²) = |a|, not a.

2. Equations with indices

  • Same base: a^f(x) = a^g(x) with a > 0, a ≠ 1 gives f(x) = g(x). Reduce both sides to one base first (4 = 2², 8 = 2³, 0.25 = 2^(−2)).
  • Same exponent: a^x = b^x (a, b > 0, a ≠ b) forces x = 0.
  • Substitution: if 2^x appears with 4^x or 2^(x+1), put t = 2^x (t > 0). 4^x − 5·2^x + 4 = 0 becomes t² − 5t + 4 = 0. Reject t ≤ 0.
  • Special cases: x^f(x) = 1 needs x = 1, or f(x) = 0, or x = −1 with f(x) even. Remember these three.

Surds (roots that are not whole numbers). √a × √b = √(ab); to remove a root from a denominator, multiply by its conjugate: 1/(√3 − √2) = (√3 + √2)/(3 − 2) = √3 + √2. To simplify a nested root, √(a + 2√b) = √p + √q where p + q = a and pq = b: √(7 + 2√10) = √5 + √2 since 5 + 2 = 7 and 5 × 2 = 10.

3. Logarithm: the definition

log_a b = c ⇔ a^c = b
Needs: base a > 0, a ≠ 1; argument b > 0

Why those conditions? A base of 1 gives 1 to every power, so the log would not be unique. A negative base gives non-real powers for fractional exponents. An argument b ≤ 0 is never reached by any positive base to any real power. Special values: log_a 1 = 0, log_a a = 1, log_a a^n = n, a^(log_a x) = x.

The laws, derived. Let log_a x = p and log_a y = q, so x = a^p and y = a^q.

log_a(xy) = p + q, because xy = a^(p+q)
log_a(x/y) = p − q, because x/y = a^(p−q)
log_a(x^n) = np, because x^n = a^(np)
log_a x = log_b x / log_b a (change of base)

Proof of change of base. If log_a x = p then a^p = x. Take log_b of both sides: p log_b a = log_b x, so p = log_b x / log_b a.

ConsequenceWhy
log_a b × log_b a = 1Change of base: (ln b/ln a)(ln a/ln b)
log_a b × log_b c = log_a cChain telescopes
log_(a^m) (b^n) = (n/m) log_a bPower rule on both sides
a^(log_b c) = c^(log_b a)Take log_b of both sides: same value
log(x) + log(y) ≠ log(x + y)Only products become sums

4. Common logs: digits and magnitude

log10 N = integer part + decimal part. For N ≥ 1, the number of digits is the integer part of log N plus 1. For 0 < N < 1, the first non-zero digit sits after (−integer part − 1) zeros. Keep log 2 = 0.3010, log 3 = 0.4771, log 7 = 0.8451, and log 5 = 1 − log 2 = 0.6990.

How many digits does 12^10 have? (log 12 = 1.0792)

10 × 1.0792 = 10.792, so 11 digits.

Where does the first non-zero digit of (0.5)^20 appear, and what is it? (log 2 = 0.3010)

log (0.5)^20 = −20 × 0.3010 = −6.02 = −7 + 0.98. So the number is 10^(−7) × 10^0.98 = 9.5 × 10^(−7). The first non-zero digit is in the 7th decimal place, and it is 9.

5. Log equations and inequalities

  1. Write the domain first (every argument > 0; base > 0, ≠ 1).
  2. Combine into one log (or move to exponent form), then solve.
  3. Reject answers that break the domain. Combining logs often widens the domain, creating extraneous roots.

Inequalities. For base a > 1, log_a x is increasing: log_a x > log_a y ⇔ x > y > 0. For 0 < a < 1 it is decreasing, so the inequality flips. log_(1/2) x > 3 means x < (1/2)³ = 1/8, with x > 0: 0 < x < 1/8.

Trap: log_2 (x − 3) < 2 gives x − 3 < 4 but also needs x − 3 > 0: so 3 < x < 7. Forgetting the lower bound is the most common slip.

6. Comparing and ordering

  • Powers of different bases: bring to the same exponent (2^300 vs 3^200: take 100th roots, 2³ = 8 vs 3² = 9, so 3^200 is larger).
  • Roots: a^(1/n) comparison: raise to the LCM of the indices. For 2^(1/2), 3^(1/3), 6^(1/6): LCM 6; 2³ = 8, 3² = 9, 6; so 3^(1/3) is the largest.
  • Logs: compare log_2 3 and log_3 5: log_2 3 = 1.585 and log_3 5 = 1.465, since 3^1.5 = 5.196 > 5.
  • The function n^(1/n) rises to a maximum at n = 3 (among integers) and then falls: 1, 1.414, 1.442, 1.414, 1.380, …

7. Traps that cost marks

  • Applying log laws to sums: log(a + b) is not log a + log b.
  • Not rejecting extraneous roots in log equations.
  • Forgetting the base-below-1 flip in log inequalities.
  • Counting digits with the integer part only.
  • Confusing log_a b with log_b a: they multiply to 1.
  • Mis-reading 2^(3^2) as (2^3)^2.

8. Worked examples

a) Simplify 8^(2/3) × 27^(−1/3) ÷ 16^(−1/2).

8^(2/3) = 4; 27^(−1/3) = 1/3; 16^(−1/2) = 1/4. So 4 × 1/3 × 4 = 16/3.

b) If a^x = b^y = (ab)^z, prove z = xy/(x + y).

Let the common value be k. a = k^(1/x), b = k^(1/y), ab = k^(1/z). So 1/x + 1/y = 1/z, so z = xy/(x + y).

c) Solve log_2(x + 1) + log_2(x − 3) = 5.

Domain: x > 3. (x + 1)(x − 3) = 32, so x² − 2x − 35 = 0, x = 7 or −5. Reject −5 (outside the domain): x = 7.

d) If log_a 3 = 2 and log_b 27 = 3, find log_(ab) 3.

log_a 3 = 2 gives a = √3 and log_b 27 = 3 gives b = 3. ab = 3√3 = 3^(3/2). So log_(ab) 3 = 1/(3/2) = 2/3.

e) Solve 3^(x+1) + 3^(x−1) = 30.

3^x (3 + 1/3) = 30 so 3^x × 10/3 = 30, 3^x = 9, x = 2.

f) Solve log_(1/3)(x − 2) ≥ −2.

Base < 1 flips the sign: x − 2 ≤ (1/3)^(−2) = 9, with x − 2 > 0. So 2 < x ≤ 11.

g) Simplify √(11 + 6√2).

Write as √(a + 2√b) with 2√b = 6√2 so √b = 3√2, b = 18. Need p + q = 11 and pq = 18: p = 9, q = 2. So √9 + √2 = 3 + √2.

The four-second check

  1. One base? Convert every number to a common base before touching the exponent.
  2. Domain? For any log, write the argument condition first.
  3. Direction: estimate to a power of the base: log_2 100 is between 6 and 7, so an answer of 3 is wrong.
  4. Trap scan: an option equal to a sum of logs or the wrong base is bait.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Telescoping log chains and equal-base equations: 40 seconds.
  2. For options that are numbers, test them in the equation instead of solving.
  3. Digit-count questions: round the product, add 1, and do not compute the number.

L12 · Coding-decoding: phrase codes and letter shuffles

Coding questions are rule detection. Compare the given pair letter by letter, write the rule in one line, test it on a second letter, and only then apply it. These questions are fast marks if you are systematic. About 30 minutes.

1. The alphabet table (write it once, use it all day)

A1B2C3D4E5F6G7H8I9J10K11L12M13
Z26Y25X24W23V22U21T20S19R18Q17P16O15N14

Mirror pairs add to 27 (A + Z, B + Y, …). "EJOTY" has positions 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, all multiples of 5. Learn these two facts and you can handle most mirror and position questions mentally.

2. Letter-level codes

Trap: a rule that fits the first letter may not fit the rest. Always verify on at least two letters, and watch for the shift changing direction midway through the word.

3. Word and phrase codes

A phrase code maps whole words to code words. You are given several coded sentences and asked for one word. The method is intersection:

  1. Find a code word that appears in two sentences. The meaning that also appears in both sentences is its partner.
  2. Remove those pairs; what is left in a sentence is the pair for the remaining code word.
  3. Check the final mapping against all given sentences.

Given: 'mil ban co' = 'dogs bark loudly', 'ban dil fo' = 'cats bark softly', 'co fo zu' = 'loudly softly always'. What is the code for 'bark'?

'ban' is common to sentences 1 and 2, and 'bark' is common to the meanings of 1 and 2 only (not 3), so ban = bark.

If a code word appears in only one sentence and so does a meaning, you cannot tell them apart without a second sentence. In that case the question will have another way to fix it. If two words never appear separately, the question is unanswerable, which happens in "Data sufficiency" variants.

4. Letter shuffles in words

  • Rearranging a word means anagram thinking: write the letters in a row and form words by common endings (-ING, -ED, -TION) first.
  • Position-based shuffles: "first and last letters swap and the middle is reversed" is just a recipe; write the word, apply each step, and double-check the length.
  • Count-based questions ("how many letters have as many letters between them as in the alphabet?") need a table: write the word with positions, check each pair.

How many pairs of letters in the word DEFAULT have as many letters between them in the word as in the alphabet?

Write D E F A U L T. Pairs that qualify: D and E (adjacent in both), E and F (adjacent in both), D and F (E lies between them in the word and in the alphabet) and D and A (E, F lie between them in the word; B, C lie between them in the alphabet: two each). Check the rest systematically: E and A have one letter between them in the word but three in the alphabet, and U, L, T do not match either. The answer is 4. List pairs in order and test each; do not scan by eye.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Write the alphabet table in the margin during the first 10 seconds of the section, not per question.
  2. Rule detection should take 20 seconds. If no single-letter pattern emerges, try positions (+, −, mirror, reverse) one at a time.
  3. Phrase codes: common-element elimination only. Do not decode all words unless needed.

L13 · Sequences and series (deep)

A sequence is a list; a series is its sum. AP adds a constant, GP multiplies by one, and almost every other pattern reduces to one of these or to a telescoping sum. We derive each formula so you can rebuild it under pressure. About 80 minutes: read for 45, examples for 20, then Drill 13.

1. Arithmetic progression (AP)

Each term is the previous plus a common difference d: a, a + d, a + 2d, …

T_n = a + (n − 1)d
S_n = n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d] = n/2 (first + last)
Mean of an AP segment = average of its first and last term

Derivation of S_n (Gauss's trick). Write the sum forwards and backwards: S = a + (a + d) + … + (a + (n − 1)d) and S = (a + (n − 1)d) + … + a. Adding column by column, every pair sums to 2a + (n − 1)d, and there are n pairs: 2S = n[2a + (n − 1)d].

  • Number of terms from first (a), last (l) and difference d: n = (l − a)/d + 1. The "+1" is the commonest slip.
  • Any term in terms of two others: T_p − T_q = (p − q)d, so d = (T_p − T_q)/(p − q).
  • Three numbers in AP: a − d, a, a + d (the sum is 3a). Four: a − 3d, a − d, a + d, a + 3d.
  • Equidistant terms: T_k + T_(n+1−k) is constant = first + last. The middle term (odd n) is the mean.
  • From S_n to T_n: T_n = S_n − S_(n−1) (n ≥ 2), T_1 = S_1. If S_n is a quadratic with no constant term, the sequence is an AP with d = 2 × coefficient of n².
  • Sum of multiples: multiples of m between two numbers form an AP with d = m.

2. Geometric progression (GP)

Each term is the previous times a common ratio r: a, ar, ar², …

T_n = a r^(n−1)
S_n = a (r^n − 1)/(r − 1) = a (1 − r^n)/(1 − r) (r ≠ 1)
S∞ = a/(1 − r), valid only for |r| < 1

Derivation of S_n. S = a + ar + … + ar^(n−1). Multiply by r: rS = ar + … + ar^n. Subtract: S − rS = a − ar^n, so S = a(1 − r^n)/(1 − r). For |r| < 1, r^n → 0 as n grows, which gives S∞ = a/(1 − r).

  • Three numbers in GP: a/r, a, ar (product = a³). Four: a/r³, a/r, ar, ar³.
  • Product of equidistant terms is constant = first × last.
  • Recurring decimals: 0.4545… = 0.45 + 0.0045 + … = 0.45/(1 − 0.01) = 5/11. Use S∞ with a = the first repeating block and r = 10^(−block length).
  • Number of terms: r^(n−1) = T_n/a. Take logs if n is not obvious, or test small powers.
  • A negative ratio alternates signs; |r| < 1 is needed for S∞ whatever its sign.
Trap: S∞ for r = 2 does not exist (the terms grow). Always check |r| < 1 before using a/(1 − r).

3. Means: AM, GM, HM

AM = (a + b)/2, GM = √(ab), HM = 2ab/(a + b) = 2/(1/a + 1/b)
GM² = AM × HM
AM ≥ GM ≥ HM for positive numbers, with equality only if a = b

Proof of AM ≥ GM: (√a − √b)² ≥ 0 gives a + b ≥ 2√(ab). Proof of GM ≥ HM: HM = GM²/AM and AM ≥ GM, so HM = GM × (GM/AM) ≤ GM.

Harmonic progression (HP): a sequence whose reciprocals form an AP. If 1/a, 1/b, 1/c are in AP then b = 2ac/(a + c) (b is the HM of a and c). To find a term of an HP, flip all terms and work in the AP.

Inserting means: n arithmetic means between a and b make an AP with n + 2 terms, so d = (b − a)/(n + 1). n geometric means make a GP with r = (b/a)^(1/(n + 1)). Only the inserted terms count in the question's sum.

4. Special series and how to get them

1 + 2 + … + n = n(n + 1)/2
1² + 2² + … + n² = n(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6
1³ + 2³ + … + n³ = [n(n + 1)/2]²
Sum of first n odd numbers = n²; sum of first n even numbers = n(n + 1)

The squares formula, derived. Use (k + 1)³ − k³ = 3k² + 3k + 1 and add for k = 1 to n: the left side telescopes to (n + 1)³ − 1. So (n + 1)³ − 1 = 3Σk² + 3Σk + n. Substitute Σk = n(n + 1)/2 and solve for Σk².

The method of differences (telescoping). When the general term can be split as f(k) − f(k + 1), nearly everything cancels. Example: 1/(k(k + 1)) = 1/k − 1/(k + 1), so the sum of n terms is 1 − 1/(n + 1) = n/(n + 1). Another: 1/(k(k + 2)) = (1/2)(1/k − 1/(k + 2)).

Products of consecutive numbers: k(k + 1) = k² + k, so Σk(k + 1) = n(n + 1)(n + 2)/3. A fast way to remember: k(k + 1)(k + 2) sums to n(n + 1)(n + 2)(n + 3)/4.

Arithmetico-geometric series (an AP multiplied term by term by a GP): multiply the sum by the ratio, subtract, and you get a pure GP. Example: S = 1 + 2x + 3x² + … gives S(1 − x) = 1 + x + x² + …, so S = 1/(1 − x)² for |x| < 1.

5. Recurrences and patterns

  • a_(n+1) = a_n + d: AP. a_(n+1) = r a_n: GP.
  • a_(n+1) = 2a_n + 1 (linear): add 1 to both sides, a_(n+1) + 1 = 2(a_n + 1), so a_n + 1 is a GP: a_n = 2^(n−1)(a₁ + 1) − 1.
  • Fibonacci type: a_(n+2) = a_(n+1) + a_n: no closed formula needed; just compute.
  • Second differences constant: the sequence is a quadratic in n. Use T_n = a n² + b n + c and solve from the first three terms.
  • Word problems: a ball dropped from height h bouncing to r h each time travels h + 2hr/(1 − r) in total. Equal monthly savings with a fixed increase is an AP. Compound growth is a GP.

6. Traps that cost marks

  • Forgetting the +1 in the number of terms.
  • Using S∞ when |r| ≥ 1.
  • Mixing up n terms and n means between two numbers.
  • Using S_n − S_(n−1) for the first term (T_1 = S_1 separately).
  • Taking the middle term of an even-length AP as a single number.
  • For HP, forgetting to flip before applying AP formulas.

7. Worked examples

a) How many terms of the AP 25, 22, 19, … must be taken so that the sum is 116?

S_n = n/2 [50 + (n − 1)(−3)] = n(53 − 3n)/2 = 116, so 3n² − 53n + 232 = 0. Discriminant 2809 − 2784 = 25, n = (53 ± 5)/6 = 9.67 or 8. n = 8. (The other root is not an integer.)

b) The sum of three numbers in GP is 14 and their product is 64. Find them.

Let them be a/r, a, ar. Product a³ = 64, so a = 4. Sum: 4/r + 4 + 4r = 14, so 4r² − 10r + 4 = 0, r = 2 or 1/2. The numbers are 2, 4, 8 (or 8, 4, 2).

c) Find the sum of 1 + 11 + 111 + … to n terms.

Each term is (10^k − 1)/9. The sum is (1/9)[(10 + 100 + … + 10^n) − n] = (1/9)[10(10^n − 1)/9 − n].

d) Write the recurring decimal 0.2323… as a fraction.

a = 0.23, r = 0.01: 0.23/0.99 = 23/99.

e) If the p-th term of an AP is q and the q-th term is p, find the (p + q)-th term.

d = (q − p)/(p − q) = −1. T_(p+q) = T_p + q × d = q − q = 0.

f) Find Σ 1/(k(k + 2)) for k = 1 to 10.

= (1/2) Σ [1/k − 1/(k + 2)] = (1/2)[1 + 1/2 − 1/11 − 1/12] = (1/2)(1.5 − 0.1742) = 0.6629. Exactly: (1/2)(3/2 − 23/132) = 175/264.

g) A ball is dropped from 80 m. Each bounce takes it to 3/4 of the previous height. Total distance before it stops?

Down 80, then up and down for each bounce: 80 + 2 × 60/(1 − 3/4) = 80 + 2 × 240 = 560 m.

The four-second check

  1. Type: constant difference (AP), constant ratio (GP), reciprocal AP (HP), or a sum that telescopes?
  2. Ask: a term, a sum, a count, or a missing parameter?
  3. Direction: an increasing AP must give a larger sum than the number of terms times the first term.
  4. Trap scan: an option off by one term, or the sum to n − 1 terms, is bait.

  2. For "find the n for a given sum" use the number of terms estimate first, then solve the quadratic.
  3. Telescoping questions: write the first three and last two terms of the split, and cross out.

L14 · Functions: domain, range, floor, modulus (deep)

A function is a rule that gives each input exactly one output. Almost every exam question is one of five tasks: find the domain, find the range, compose, invert, or solve a functional equation. For each we give a method that works every time. About 80 minutes: read for 45, examples for 20, then Drill 14.

1. What a function is

A function f from set A (the domain) to set B (the codomain) assigns to each element of A exactly one element of B. The set of outputs actually produced is the range (a subset of the codomain).

  • Vertical line test: a graph is a function if every vertical line meets it at most once. x² + y² = 1 fails; y = ±√x is not a function.
  • One-one (injective): different inputs give different outputs; the horizontal line test: every horizontal line meets the graph at most once.
  • Onto (surjective): range = codomain. Every output in B is hit.
  • Bijective: both. Only a bijection has an inverse.
  • Counting (finite sets): with |A| = m, |B| = n: functions n^m; one-one functions n!/(n − m)! (needs n ≥ m); bijections n! when m = n.

2. Finding the domain

The domain is the intersection of all conditions. List each, solve each, then intersect. If a function is built from pieces, the domain of each piece must be satisfied.

Trap: simplifying before reading the domain. f(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1) equals x + 1 but its domain still excludes x = 1.

3. Finding the range

  1. Inverse thinking: solve y = f(x) for x. The values of y for which x exists in the domain are the range. This is the most reliable method.
  2. Quadratics: complete the square (vertex value) and note the open direction. y = x² − 4x + 7 = (x − 2)² + 3 has range [3, ∞).
  3. Rational functions y = (ax² + bx + c)/(dx² + ex + f): cross-multiply to get a quadratic in x, then demand a real root: discriminant ≥ 0 gives an inequality in y.
  4. Bounded building blocks: −1 ≤ sin x, cos x ≤ 1; |x| ≥ 0; x² ≥ 0; e^x > 0. Combine them by interval arithmetic.
  5. Floor and fractional part: ⌊x⌋ takes all integers; {x} = x − ⌊x⌋ lies in [0, 1).

Range of y = x/(x² + 1).

yx² − x + y = 0. For real x, discriminant 1 − 4y² ≥ 0, so −1/2 ≤ y ≤ 1/2. (For y = 0, x = 0 works.) Range [−1/2, 1/2].

4. Composition and inverse

(f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)): apply g first
f ∘ g ≠ g ∘ f in general; (f ∘ g) ∘ h = f ∘ (g ∘ h)
Domain of f ∘ g: x in the domain of g, with g(x) in the domain of f

Inverse. If f is a bijection with f(x) = y, then f⁻¹(y) = x. To find it: write y = f(x), solve for x, then swap names.

  • f(f⁻¹(x)) = x and f⁻¹(f(x)) = x on the right domains.
  • (f ∘ g)⁻¹ = g⁻¹ ∘ f⁻¹ (undo in the reverse order).
  • The graph of f⁻¹ is the reflection of f in the line y = x. A point (a, b) on f gives (b, a) on f⁻¹.
  • To evaluate f⁻¹(k) quickly, solve f(x) = k rather than finding the formula.
  • An involution satisfies f = f⁻¹: f(x) = (x + 1)/(x − 1) is one, since f(f(x)) = x.

5. Even, odd, periodic, monotone

  • Even: f(−x) = f(x) (symmetric about the y-axis): x², |x|, cos x. Odd: f(−x) = −f(x) (symmetric about the origin): x³, sin x, x|x|.
  • Even × even = even; odd × odd = even; even × odd = odd. Any f can be split into even and odd parts: f(x) = [f(x) + f(−x)]/2 + [f(x) − f(−x)]/2.
  • Periodic with period T: f(x + T) = f(x). sin x has period 2π; |sin x| has period π; {x} has period 1.
  • Increasing: x₁ < x₂ ⇒ f(x₁) < f(x₂). A function that is strictly monotone is one-one.

6. Floor, ceiling, fractional part, modulus

⌊x⌋ = greatest integer ≤ x; ⌈x⌉ = least integer ≥ x
x = ⌊x⌋ + {x}, 0 ≤ {x} < 1
⌊−x⌋ = −⌊x⌋ − 1 for non-integer x; = −⌊x⌋ for integer x
⌊x + n⌋ = ⌊x⌋ + n for integer n
  • Solving ⌊g(x)⌋ = n: n ≤ g(x) < n + 1. Solve the double inequality.
  • Equations like ⌊x⌋ = 2x − 3: let ⌊x⌋ = n, express x in n, and require n ≤ x < n + 1: this fixes which integers n are possible.
  • Floor of a negative: ⌊−2.3⌋ = −3, not −2.
  • Modulus function: |x| = x for x ≥ 0, −x for x < 0; graphs are V-shaped; |f(x)| reflects the negative part of f above the axis.
  • Graph shifts: f(x − a) shifts right by a; f(x) + b shifts up by b; −f(x) flips in the x-axis; f(−x) flips in the y-axis; f(ax) compresses horizontally by a.

7. Functional equations: four standard moves

  1. Special values. Put x = 0, 1, −1 or y = 0 to extract f(0), f(1).
  2. Swap and solve. For af(x) + bf(1/x) = g(x), replace x by 1/x to get a second equation and eliminate f(1/x).
  3. f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y) (with a normal condition) gives f(x) = kx, with k = f(1). f(x + y) = f(x)f(y) with f > 0 gives f(x) = a^x with a = f(1).
  4. Build up. If f(x + 1) = f(x) + 3 and f(0) = 2, then f(n) = 2 + 3n: compute a few values and spot the AP or GP.
Trap: after solving, check the answer in the original equation. A functional equation can be satisfied only by a specific f, and any f you guess must satisfy it for all inputs, not just one.

8. Traps that cost marks

  • Composing in the wrong order: f(g(x)) means g first.
  • Simplifying first and losing a domain restriction.
  • Taking the codomain as the range.
  • Forgetting that ⌊−2.3⌋ = −3.
  • Branch errors in piecewise functions: test the condition at every step of a nested evaluation.
  • Writing f⁻¹(x) = 1/f(x). The inverse function is not the reciprocal.

9. Worked examples

a) Domain of f(x) = √(4 − x²)/ln(x + 1).

4 − x² ≥ 0: −2 ≤ x ≤ 2. x + 1 > 0: x > −1. Denominator ln(x + 1) ≠ 0: x ≠ 0. Intersect: (−1, 0) ∪ (0, 2].

b) f(x) = 3x − 5, g(x) = x² + 1. Find x if (f ∘ g)(x) = (g ∘ f)(x).

f(g(x)) = 3x² − 2. g(f(x)) = (3x − 5)² + 1 = 9x² − 30x + 26. Set equal: 6x² − 30x + 28 = 0, 3x² − 15x + 14 = 0, x = [15 ± √(225 − 168)]/6 = [15 ± √57]/6.

c) If f(x) = (x + 1)/(x − 1), find f(f(x)).

f(f(x)) = ((x + 1)/(x − 1) + 1)/((x + 1)/(x − 1) − 1) = (2x/(x − 1))/(2/(x − 1)) = x. So f is its own inverse.

d) Number of one-one functions from {1, 2, 3} to {a, b, c, d, e}.

5 × 4 × 3 = 60.

e) Solve ⌊2x − 1⌋ = 3.

3 ≤ 2x − 1 < 4 ⇒ 4 ≤ 2x < 5 ⇒ 2 ≤ x < 2.5.

f) f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y) and f(3) = 12. Find f(7).

f(x) = kx with k = f(3)/3 = 4, so f(7) = 28.

g) Range of f(x) = (x² − 3x + 2)/(x² + x + 1).

y(x² + x + 1) = x² − 3x + 2 ⇒ (y − 1)x² + (y + 3)x + (y − 2) = 0. For y ≠ 1, discriminant (y + 3)² − 4(y − 1)(y − 2) ≥ 0 ⇒ −3y² + 18y + 1 ≥ 0 ⇒ 3y² − 18y − 1 ≤ 0 ⇒ (9 − 2√21)/3 ≤ y ≤ (9 + 2√21)/3, and y = 1 gives 4x − 1 = 0, which has a root, so 1 is included (it lies in the interval).

The four-second check

  1. Task: domain, range, composition, inverse or functional equation?
  2. Conditions: list every restriction (denominator, even root, log, branch) before simplifying.
  3. Direction: composition runs inside-out. Test your formula at one value.
  4. Trap scan: an option equal to the codomain, the reciprocal or the composition in the wrong order is bait.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Plug in a test value to check any formula you derived: x = 0, 1 or 2.
  2. For range questions with options, test whether the end values can be hit.
  3. Floor questions: write n ≤ g(x) < n + 1 and solve; do not guess.
  4. Piecewise composition: write the branch next to each value as you go.
