L19 · Set theory (deep)

Sets are the language under Venn-diagram word problems, probability and relations. We build the vocabulary, derive inclusion-exclusion rather than memorise it, and then learn the counting tricks on subsets that the IIM-B paper likes. About 60 minutes.

In CAT-style English, "or" means union (at least one), "and" means intersection, "neither" means outside the union, and "only A" means A minus everything else.

2. Two sets: derive the formula

Adding n(A) and n(B) counts everything in the overlap twice, so subtract it once:

n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)
n(neither) = N − n(A ∪ B)
n(exactly one) = n(A) + n(B) − 2 n(A ∩ B)

Of 60 students, 35 play cricket, 28 play football and 10 play both. How many play neither?

Union = 35 + 28 − 10 = 53, so neither = 60 − 53 = 7. Only cricket: 35 − 10 = 25. Only football: 18. Check: 25 + 18 + 10 + 7 = 60.

The four-region habit. Draw two overlapping circles and fill the middle first. Then each "only" region is the circle total minus the middle, and the outside is the remainder. Filling the middle first is the single habit that prevents most errors.

Trap: "35 play cricket" includes those who also play football. It is the whole circle, not the "only" part. Questions that say "35 play only cricket" are different, and the middle is then added separately.

3. Three sets

n(A ∪ B ∪ C) = n(A) + n(B) + n(C) − n(A∩B) − n(B∩C) − n(A∩C) + n(A∩B∩C)

Why: the triple overlap is counted three times by the singles, subtracted three times by the pairs (so it vanishes), and must be added back once. Each region of the Venn diagram then ends up counted exactly once.

Three derived formulas save the day when the question asks "exactly":

Exactly two = Σ(pairs) − 3 × (all three)
Exactly one = Σ(singles) − 2 × Σ(pairs) + 3 × (all three)
Exactly three = all three

100 people. 50 like A, 45 like B, 40 like C. 20 like A and B, 15 like B and C, 18 like A and C, and 8 like all three. Find the number who like at least one, exactly one, exactly two, and none.

Union = 135 − 53 + 8 = 90. Exactly two = 53 − 24 = 29 (check by region: AB only 12, BC only 7, AC only 10). Exactly one = 135 − 106 + 24 = 53 (A only 20, B only 18, C only 15). All three = 8. Total 53 + 29 + 8 = 90. None = 10.

The seven-region method. In a three-circle diagram fill the centre first (all three), then each pair-only region (pair total minus centre), then each single-only region (circle total minus the three regions inside it). It is slower than the formulas but it never needs memory, and it shows you which region the question refers to.

4. Maximum and minimum overlap

When only the totals are given, the overlap can vary. Two limits always hold: the overlap cannot exceed the smaller set, and the union cannot exceed the whole group.

Max overlap = smaller set
Min overlap = n(A) + n(B) − N (if this is positive; otherwise 0)

70% of a town drinks tea and 80% drinks coffee. Everyone drinks at least one. What are the least and greatest percentages who drink both?

Least: 70 + 80 − 100 = 50%. Greatest: 70% (every tea-drinker also drinks coffee). So the overlap is between 50% and 70%.

Trap: if the question says "everyone likes at least one", the union equals N, so the overlap is fixed: n(A) + n(B) − N. The range appears only when someone may like neither.

5. Counting subsets

  • A set with n elements has 2^n subsets, because each element is independently in or out.
  • Proper subsets exclude the set itself: 2^n − 1. Non-empty proper: 2^n − 2.
  • Subsets containing k specified elements: fix them in, the rest choose freely: 2^(n−k).
  • Subsets containing a specified element but excluding another: 2^(n−2).
  • Subsets with exactly r elements: C(n, r). Subsets of even size: 2^(n−1), and odd size: 2^(n−1), for n ≥ 1, because C(n,0) + C(n,2) + ... = C(n,1) + C(n,3) + ...

A set has 9 elements. How many subsets contain three particular elements?

Fix those three in. The other 6 are free: 2^6 = 64.

A set has 10 elements. How many of its subsets have an even number of elements?

Half of all subsets: 2^10 / 2 = 512. Reason: toggling the first element changes the parity and pairs every even-sized subset with an odd-sized one.

6. Subsets with no two consecutive elements

Let f(n) be the number of subsets of {1,...,n} with no two consecutive numbers (the empty set included). Either n is not in the subset (f(n−1) ways) or n is in it, so n−1 is out (f(n−2) ways). So f(n) = f(n−1) + f(n−2): a Fibonacci recursion with f(1) = 2 and f(2) = 3.

Learn the pattern, not the row. Extend it yourself to any n by adding the last two entries: you can rebuild the row in 20 seconds from f(1) = 2, f(2) = 3.

7. Number-theory sets: inclusion-exclusion on multiples

The number of integers from 1 to N divisible by d is ⌊N/d⌋. For "divisible by a or b", subtract the multiples of lcm(a, b), not of a × b, unless they are coprime.

How many integers from 1 to 100 are divisible by 2 or 3? By 2, 3 or 5?

2 or 3: 50 + 33 − 16 = 67 (multiples of 6: 16). 2, 3 or 5: 50 + 33 + 20 − 16 − 10 − 6 + 3 = 74 (multiples of 6, 10, 15: 16, 10, 6; of 30: 3). Neither of these: 100 − 74 = 26.

This is exactly the three-set formula with "likes A" replaced by "divisible by 2".

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Draw the circles before reading the numbers twice. Fill the middle first.
  2. "Exactly" and "only" are different from "at least". Underline which one is asked.
  3. Percent problems: set N = 100 and use the same formulas.
  4. Subset questions: ask "what is fixed in, what is fixed out, what is free?" and then use 2 to the power free.
  5. Finish by checking that your regions add to N.

L20 · Relations (deep)

A relation is just a chosen collection of ordered pairs. Everything else (reflexive, symmetric, transitive, equivalence, functions) is a property of that collection, and the counting questions follow from counting the free choices. About 60 minutes.

1. Cartesian product and relations

A × B is the set of all ordered pairs (a, b) with a in A and b in B. It has n(A) × n(B) pairs. Order matters: (1, 2) ≠ (2, 1), and A × B ≠ B × A in general.

A relation from A to B is any subset of A × B. So a relation is a yes/no choice for each pair in A × B, which gives:

Number of relations from A to B = 2^(n(A) × n(B))

A has 2 elements and B has 4. How many relations are there from A to B?

A × B has 8 pairs, each in or out: 2^8 = 256.

TermMeaningFor R = {(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)} from {1,2,3} to {1,2,3}
DomainFirst coordinates that actually appear{1, 2}
CodomainThe target set B (given, not computed){1, 2, 3}
RangeSecond coordinates that actually appear{2, 3}
Inverse R⁻¹Reverse every pair{(2,1),(3,1),(3,2)}

R = {(x, y) : y = x² − 1, x ∈ {−3, ..., 3}}. How many elements are in the range?

y values: 8, 3, 0, −1, 0, 3, 8. Distinct: {−1, 0, 3, 8}, so 4. The range counts distinct values, not pairs.

2. The three properties (on a set A, relation R ⊆ A × A)

PropertyMeaningMatrix view
Reflexive(a, a) ∈ R for every aWhole diagonal filled
Symmetric(a, b) ∈ R implies (b, a) ∈ RMatrix equals its mirror image
Transitive(a, b) and (b, c) in R imply (a, c) in RPath of length 2 must also be a direct pair
Antisymmetric(a, b) and (b, a) in R imply a = bNo mirrored pair off the diagonal

An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. A partial order is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive.

Traps: (1) Symmetric and antisymmetric are not opposites: the identity relation is both. (2) Transitivity is checked by every chain, including chains that use the same pair twice. A relation with no chains (for example {(1,2)}) is transitive for free. (3) A relation can be symmetric and transitive but not reflexive if some element has no pairs at all.

On A = {1, 2, 3}, R = {(1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (1,2), (2,1)}. Is R reflexive, symmetric, transitive?

Reflexive: the diagonal is complete, yes. Symmetric: (1,2) has (2,1) and the diagonal pairs are their own mirrors, yes. Transitive: the chains (1,2),(2,1) give (1,1); (2,1),(1,2) give (2,2); every chain through a diagonal pair returns the same pair. All present, so yes. R is an equivalence relation with classes {1,2} and {3}.

Test on real relations.

  • "a divides b" on positive integers: reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive, not symmetric (2 divides 4 but 4 does not divide 2). A partial order.
  • "|a − b| ≤ 1" on integers: reflexive, symmetric, not transitive (1 and 2, 2 and 3, but 1 and 3 differ by 2).
  • "a − b is divisible by 5": equivalence relation (difference of equals is 0; negate a multiple of 5; add two multiples of 5).
  • "a is a brother of b": usually not symmetric (a may be a brother of a sister), never reflexive.

3. Counting relations on an n-element set

The n² pairs split into n diagonal pairs and n² − n off-diagonal pairs. n(n − 1)/2 of the off-diagonal pairs are mirror partners.

How many reflexive relations are there on a 5-element set?

Diagonal forced. The other 25 − 5 = 20 pairs are free: 2^20.

How many relations on a 4-element set are both reflexive and symmetric?

Diagonal forced. The 6 mirror pairs (choose 2 of 4) are each in or out as a pair: 2^6 = 64.

How many symmetric relations are there on a 4-element set?

4 diagonal pairs free, 6 mirror pairs free: 2^10 = 1024.

4. Equivalence relations, classes and partitions

An equivalence relation splits the set into equivalence classes that do not overlap and together cover everything (a partition). Conversely, every partition gives exactly one equivalence relation (“in the same block”). So:

Number of equivalence relations on n elements = number of ways to partition the set
n = 1, 2, 3 gives 1, 2, 5. For larger n, count partitions by block pattern.

How many equivalence relations are there on {a, b, c}?

Count partitions by block pattern: one block of 3 (1 way); 2 + 1 (3 ways, choose the single); 1 + 1 + 1 (1 way). Total 1 + 3 + 1 = 5. For four elements you add the patterns 4, 3 + 1, 2 + 2, 2 + 1 + 1 and 1 + 1 + 1 + 1, taking care that 2 + 2 splits the four in 3 ways, not 6.

On the integers, aRb if a − b is divisible by 7. How many classes?

Each integer leaves remainder 0, 1, ..., 6 when divided by 7, and two integers are related exactly when the remainders match. So there are 7 classes.

On {1,...,12}, aRb means a − b is divisible by 3. Class sizes?

Related exactly when the remainders mod 3 match. Remainder 1: {1,4,7,10}; remainder 2: {2,5,8,11}; remainder 0: {3,6,9,12}. Sizes 4, 4, 4, which add to 12.

5. Composition and counting pairs

R ∘ R (apply R twice) contains (a, c) whenever there is some b with (a, b) ∈ R and (b, c) ∈ R. List chains: first pair's second element must equal the second pair's first element.

R = {(1,2), (2,3), (2,4), (3,5)}. How many pairs are in R ∘ R?

Chains: (1,2) then (2,3) gives (1,3); (1,2) then (2,4) gives (1,4); (2,3) then (3,5) gives (2,5). The pairs ending in 4 or 5 have no continuation. So R ∘ R = {(1,3), (1,4), (2,5)}: 3 pairs.

On {1,...,10}, R is "a divides b". How many ordered pairs?

For each a, count its multiples up to 10: ⌊10/a⌋. Sum: 10 + 5 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 27.

6. Relations that are functions; counting maps

A function from A to B is a relation in which every element of A appears exactly once as a first coordinate. With n(A) = m and n(B) = n:

All functions: n^m
One-one (needs m ≤ n): n × (n−1) × ... × (n−m+1)
Onto (m ≥ n): n^m − C(n,1)(n−1)^m + C(n,2)(n−2)^m − ...

How many onto functions are there from {1,...,5} to {a, b}?

All maps: 2^5 = 32. A map fails to be onto only if it sends everything to a single target, and there are 2 such maps. So 32 − 2 = 30. With the formula: 2^5 − C(2,1) × 1^5 = 30. For 3 targets you also subtract the maps that miss each one target and add back those that miss two, so the full formula is 3^m − 3 × 2^m + 3 × 1^m.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Counting questions: write the pairs as a picture, split into diagonal and mirror pairs, then count free choices.
  2. Property questions: test reflexive first (fastest), then symmetric, then check every chain for transitivity.
  3. Classes: find the invariant (remainder, parity, same sign) that defines "related".
  4. Check "equivalence classes" count by making sure sizes add up to the set size.

L22 · Permutations and combinations (deep)

Every counting problem is built from three moves: multiply for sequential choices, add for exclusive cases, and divide to remove overcounting. We build the tools from the multiplication principle, then solve the standard IIM-B families: restrictions, repeats, circular, distributions and geometry. About 60 minutes.

1. The two principles

Multiplication: a task done in stages with m, n, p ... choices gives m × n × p ... ways.
Addition: if the cases are exclusive, total = case 1 + case 2 + ...

Decision rule: if the question says "and then", multiply. If it says "either ... or" with no overlap, add. Most hard questions need both: split into cases, multiply inside each case, add the cases.

n! = n × (n−1) × ... × 1, with 0! = 1
P(n, r) = n!/(n−r)! (ordered selection)
C(n, r) = n!/(r!(n−r)!) (unordered selection); C(n, r) = C(n, n−r)

Why C = P / r!: each unordered group of r can be ordered in r! ways, and P counts all orderings.

2. Arrangements with repeated letters

If some objects are identical, divide by the factorial of each repeat count: n! / (p! q! r! ...).

Arrangements of BALLOON?

7 letters, L twice and O twice: 7!/(2! 2!) = 5040/4 = 1260.

Arrangements of ENGINEERING?

11 letters: E × 3, N × 3, G × 2, I × 2, R × 1. 11!/(3! 3! 2! 2!) = 39916800/144 = 277200.

Rank problems (position of a word in the dictionary) follow the same logic: count words that start with each smaller letter, then move to the next position.

3. Restrictions: three standard moves

  • Glue ("together"): treat the group as one object, arrange, then arrange inside the group.
  • Gap method ("not together", "no two adjacent"): arrange the free objects first, then place the restricted objects in the gaps between and around them.
  • Complement: total minus the unwanted. Use it whenever "at least" or "not all" appears.

Seven different books are arranged on a shelf. In how many arrangements are four particular books NOT all together?

Total 7! = 5040. All four together: glue them, giving 4 objects (4! = 24), times 4! = 24 inner orders: 576. Answer 5040 − 576 = 4464.

Arrangements of BALLOON with no two L's together?

Place the non-L letters B, A, O, O, N first: 5!/2! = 60 ways. They make 6 gaps (before, between, after). Choose 2 gaps for the L's: C(6,2) = 15. Total 60 × 15 = 900.

Trap: in the glue method, forgetting the inside order (the 3! above) halves your answer or worse. In the gap method, the number of gaps is (objects + 1), not (objects).

4. Number formation with digit conditions

Fill the most restricted position first. A leading zero is never allowed, so handle the zero case separately when 0 is available.

Three-digit odd numbers with distinct digits from {1,...,7}?

Units digit odd: 4 choices (1, 3, 5, 7). Remaining two places from the other 6 digits: 6 × 5 = 30. Total 4 × 30 = 120.

Three-digit numbers divisible by 5 from the digits 0 to 6 with no repetition?

Units digit 0: the other two places from 6 digits: 6 × 5 = 30. Units digit 5: hundreds digit cannot be 0 or 5, so 5 choices; tens digit from the 5 left (0 is now allowed): 5 × 5 = 25. Total 55.

Whenever 0 is allowed and the number has a restricted leading digit, split the cases by what the restricted position uses.

5. Selections: committees and "at least"

A committee of 5 from 6 men and 4 women, with at least 2 women?

Use the complement. Total C(10,5) = 252. Zero women: C(6,5) = 6. One woman: 4 × C(6,4) = 60. At least 2 women = 252 − 6 − 60 = 186.

10 people shake hands pairwise, except that four particular people do not shake hands among themselves. How many handshakes?

C(10,2) = 45, minus the C(4,2) = 6 handshakes among the four: 39.

Trap: selecting people for different roles (captain, vice-captain) is ordered, so use P or multiply stages. Selecting a team is unordered.

6. Circular arrangements

In a circle only relative positions matter, so fix one person and arrange the rest: (n − 1)! ways. If clockwise and anticlockwise count as the same (a necklace or garland), divide by 2.

8 people sit round a table. In how many ways do two particular people sit adjacent? Not adjacent?

Adjacent: glue the pair, 7 objects in a circle, (7 − 1)! = 720, times 2 inner orders = 1440. Total circular arrangements are 7! = 5040, so not adjacent = 5040 − 1440 = 3600.

7. Distributions and integer solutions (stars and bars)

Distributing n identical items among k people:

Each gets 0 or more: C(n + k − 1, k − 1)
Each gets at least 1: C(n − 1, k − 1)
Each gets at least m: substitute y = x − m, so the sum becomes n − km and the first formula applies

12 identical chocolates to 5 children, each at least 1?

C(11,4) = 330.

Integer solutions of x + y + z + w = 20 with each at least 2?

Let each variable be reduced by 2. The sum becomes 20 − 8 = 12 with each at least 0: C(12 + 3, 3) = C(15,3) = 455.

Distinct items to distinct people are different: each item has k choices, giving k^n.

8. Geometry counts

  • Lines from n points, no three collinear: C(n, 2). Triangles: C(n, 3).
  • Diagonals of an n-gon: C(n, 2) − n = n(n − 3)/2. A 12-sided polygon has 12 × 9 / 2 = 54.
  • If k of the points are collinear, subtract the overcount: triangles = C(n,3) − C(k,3).

  1. Name the family in 5 seconds: repeats, restriction, selection, circular, distribution, geometry.
  2. "At least" and "not together": complement. "Together": glue. "No two adjacent": gaps.
  3. If the number is small (under about 20), list or count by cases to check.
  4. Sanity-check: your answer must be smaller than the unrestricted total.

L15 · Blood relations

Blood-relation questions are chain problems. The method is to draw a small family tree, one generation per line, and to translate every phrase into a gender and a generation before you answer. About 30 minutes.

1. The vocabulary table

2. Method: tree first, answer second

  1. Start from the person the question asks about, and walk through the statements in order.
  2. Write each person as a letter with a gender mark (M, F or ?) and place generations on separate lines.
  3. Use a horizontal line for siblings or spouses and a vertical line for parent to child.
  4. At each step, write what you know and what you do not know. If gender is unknown, "cousin" or "sibling" stays unspecified.
  5. Translate the final path into one word, then match the options.

A is B's brother. C is A's mother. D is C's mother. How is B related to D?

A and B are siblings, so C is also B's mother. D is C's mother, so D is B's maternal grandmother and B is D's grandchild. B's gender is not given, so "grandson or granddaughter" is as far as the chain goes.

Trap: stopping at the first plausible relation. Always carry the chain to the final question, and watch whether the link is on the mother's or the father's side.

3. Photograph and "my" questions

"Pointing to a woman, a man says: she is the only daughter of my father's only son." My father's only son is me. So the woman is my daughter. Work backwards from the "only" phrase before anything else: "only son of my father" is the speaker himself, "only daughter of my mother" is the speaker herself.

"Her father's mother's only son": the father's mother is the woman's paternal grandmother, and her only son is the woman's father. Go one link at a time and write each person on the tree.

4. Coded relations

Symbols such as "P + Q" mean P is the mother of Q. Decode each symbol into a relation first, build the tree from the left, and check which side of the family the final person is on.

'P × Q' means P is father of Q, 'P + Q' means P is mother of Q. What does A × B + C mean?

A is B's father. B is C's mother. So A is the father of C's mother: C's maternal grandfather.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Spend 10 seconds writing the tree, with gender marks.
  2. Do not guess gender from the name. If the question does not say, keep the relation neutral (cousin, sibling, spouse).
  3. For in-laws, write the spouse as a separate node linked to the person.
  4. Cross out options that fail on gender or generation first.

L16 · Paragraph summary and odd sentence out

Both question types test whether you can find the spine of a short paragraph. Paragraph summary asks for the spine in one sentence, and odd-sentence-out asks which sentence is not on it. About 30 minutes.

1. Paragraph summary: four steps

  1. Skeleton: in 10 seconds, write one line: what is the topic and what is the claim? Look for the turn: but, yet, however, though. The claim is usually after the turn.
  2. Predict the summary in your own words before reading the options.
  3. Cross out by shape: (a) too extreme (always, never, should stop), (b) out of scope (introduces a new idea), (c) too narrow (only one example), (d) opposite or distorted.
  4. Keep the best match: it includes the main claim and any key qualifier the author added.

"Electric cars emit nothing from the exhaust, so cities breathe easier. But in many countries the electricity that charges them comes largely from coal, so the gain depends on how the grid is powered."

Skeleton: electric cars cut local pollution, but the total benefit depends on the power source. An option saying "electric cars are no better than petrol cars" distorts the passage, and one saying "electric cars always reduce pollution" ignores the turn after "but".

Trap: the qualifier. In "exercise improves mood, though only if it is regular", an option that drops the "though" part is incomplete even though it sounds right.

2. Odd sentence out: how to find the intruder

  1. Read all five sentences once, quickly. Decide the topic of the paragraph.
  2. Mark any sentence that seems to be about something else. Test it: if you remove it, does the paragraph still run smoothly?
  3. Check connectors: a sentence that begins with "therefore", "this" or "it" needs a clear referent in the earlier sentence. If the referent is missing, it belongs to a different thread.
  4. The odd sentence is often true and interesting but about a related, not the same, subject (honey for bees, cricket for the monsoon). Do not keep a sentence just because it sounds sensible.

1. Tea reached Europe through Dutch traders in the early 1600s. 2. By the next century it had become Britain's national drink. 3. Demand was so large that the East India Company traded it at scale. 4. Football was formalised in England in the 1860s. 5. Heavy taxes on tea later led to widespread smuggling.

Sentences 1, 2, 3 and 5 follow tea through trade, demand and taxation. Sentence 4 is true, but it is about football and breaks the thread.

Trap: if two sentences look odd, one of them is usually part of a link (for example, an example that supports the sentence before). Ask which one is needed for the chain.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Summary: 20 seconds to read, 10 to predict, 30 to cross out.
  2. Odd sentence: find the topic from the first and last sentences, then test the middle ones.
  3. Never pick an option because it uses words from the passage. Wrong options copy words too.

L17 · Number and letter series

A series is a rule applied to positions. Find the rule by testing a short list of families in a fixed order and verifying on every term, not just the first few. About 30 minutes.

1. The test order

TestWhat you seeExample
DifferencesConstant (AP), or differences themselves in AP or GP2, 5, 11, 20, 32: differences 3, 6, 9, 12, next 15 gives 47
RatiosConstant (GP), or ratio changes by a fixed step3, 9, 27, 81
Doubling differencesDifferences 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 ...3, 4, 6, 10, 18, 34: next difference 32 gives 66
Squares and cubesn², n² ± k, n³ ± n2, 5, 10, 17: n² + 1
Multiply and addt(n+1) = a × t(n) + b2, 4, 10, 28, 82: × 3 − 2
InterleavedTwo series in alternate places2, 50, 4, 45, 8, 40, 16, ?: odd positions × 2, even positions − 5, so next is 35
Primes, FibonacciEach term is the sum of the previous two, or prime numbers2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21
Trap: a rule that fits the first four terms but breaks on the fifth. Always run the rule across every given term before choosing.

2. Letter series

Convert letters to positions (A = 1, ... Z = 26) and treat them as numbers. Mirror letters add to 27. In a group series such as AZ, BY, DW, GT, look at the first letters and second letters as two separate number series.

CX, EV, HS, LO, ?

First letters: 3, 5, 8, 12: steps +2, +3, +4, so next is +5 = 17 (Q). Second letters: 24, 22, 19, 15: steps −2, −3, −4, so next is 15 − 5 = 10 (J). Check the mirror: 3 + 24 = 27. The answer is QJ.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Write differences first. If they are not constant, write second differences or ratios.
  2. If nothing works in 20 seconds, test interleaving (two separate series).
  3. Always verify the rule on all terms, then compute the next term, then match the options.

L18 · Data interpretation: tables and charts

IIM-B QADI sets use tables and charts with 3 to 5 questions each, and the questions are mostly percentage change, share, ratio and comparison. Read the labels first, choose a few clean calculations, and approximate where the options allow it. About 40 minutes.

1. Read the chart before the question

  1. Read the title, units (thousands? crores? percent?) and axes. A wrong unit ruins everything.
  2. Note what each series is (for a bar chart, which colour is which year or product).
  3. Skim the questions for which rows or years are needed, so you only read those off.

2. The four calculations

Percentage change = (new − old) / old × 100
Share = part / total × 100
Ratio a : b = a / b (simplify before comparing)
Average = total / number of items
Traps: (1) percentage change is relative to the old value, not the new. (2) a rise from 40 to 60 is +50%, but a fall from 60 to 40 is only −33.3%. (3) "percentage points" and "percent" differ: from 20% to 25% is +5 percentage points, or +25% relative.

Sales rise from 80 to 100 in one year. What is the growth?

(100 − 80) / 80 = 25%.

3. Speed-ups

  • Compare before you compute: to find which region has the larger share, compare fractions by cross-multiplying, or round to simple numbers.
  • Use ratios to skip totals: if A is 3 and B is 4 in a ratio, the total is 7 parts.
  • Approximate when options are far apart: 48 out of 197 is about 24%. Exact division is wasted when the options are 20%, 24%, 30%, 35%.
  • Estimate bar heights to the nearest gridline and keep one decimal.
  • Skip the question that needs more than three calculations. In a set of four, take the cheap ones first.

4. Table versus chart questions

Tables give exact numbers, so the work is arithmetic. Charts give approximate numbers, so the work is reading. If an option differs from the exact answer by less than your reading error, the question is flawed, but in IIM-B papers the options are usually well separated.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Spend the first 30 seconds on the set reading labels and units.
  2. Mark the rows or bars each question needs. Compute only those.
  3. Write intermediate numbers beside the chart so you can reuse them in the next question.

L21 · Ranking and scheduling

Ranking questions reduce to one formula. Scheduling puzzles are linear arrangements wearing a calendar: build a grid of people against days and fill it clue by clue. About 40 minutes.

1. Ranking

Total = (rank from top) + (rank from bottom) − 1
Rank from the other end = Total − rank + 1
People between two persons in the same direction = |rank1 − rank2| − 1

Meera is 9th from the top and 25th from the bottom. How many students are in the class?

9 + 25 − 1 = 33. Meera is counted in both ranks, so subtract 1.

In a class of 50, Kabir is 18th from the top. His rank from the bottom?

50 − 18 + 1 = 33.

In a class of 36, Isha is 10th from the top. After a test she moves down 5 places. Her new rank from the bottom?

New rank from the top is 15. From the bottom: 36 − 15 + 1 = 22.

Traps: (1) forgetting the +1 when converting ends. (2) "moves down 8 places" increases the rank from the top by 8 and decreases the rank from the bottom by 8. (3) between two people is |a − b| − 1, not |a − b|.

2. Scheduling puzzles

Six people present on six days, one per day. This is a row of six where positions are days. Use the same toolkit as linear arrangements:

  1. Draw a strip with six boxes labelled Mon to Sat.
  2. Place fixed clues first ("X presents on Wednesday"). Then use "the day after" as a chained pair that slides along the strip.
  3. "Exactly two days between" fixes two positions that are three apart: (1,4), (2,5) or (3,6).
  4. "Before" clues are weaker; keep them in a side list and use them to eliminate positions.
  5. Count remaining possibilities after each clue. Stop when each person has one box.
Trap: "the day after" means immediately after, a pair (n, n+1). "Before" means anywhere earlier. Mixing the two is the most common mistake.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Spend the first two minutes on the set: place the fixed and chained clues, and mark what is still free.
  2. Answer the questions from the filled strip, not from the clues.
  3. Check the final strip against all clues once before answering.
