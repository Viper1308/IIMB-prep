L1 · Percentages, profit and loss, discount

Every question in this lesson is the same question: percent of what? Most lost marks come from the wrong base, not from hard arithmetic.

1. The base decides everything

A percent is a fraction of some base. When the base changes, the same percent means a different amount. A 20% rise followed by a 20% fall does not return to the start, because the fall is taken on a bigger number.

Increase by p%: multiply by (1 + p/100)
Decrease by p%: multiply by (1 - p/100)
Successive changes: multiply the multipliers
Net of two changes a% then b%: a + b + ab/100
(use a negative number for a fall)

2. Switching the base

A is x% more than B → B is x/(100+x) × 100 % less than A
A is x% less than B → B is x/(100-x) × 100 % more than A
Price up x% → cut consumption by x/(100+x) × 100 % to keep spend equal

Check with numbers: 80 to 60 is a 25% fall. To go back from 60 to 80 you need a 33.33% rise.

Mental shortcuts: 10% is a shift of the decimal point; 5% is half of that; 1% is two places over; 15% = 10% + 5%.

4. Profit, loss and discount

Profit % = (SP - CP)/CP × 100 (on cost unless told otherwise)
SP = CP × (1 + p/100) or CP × (1 - l/100)
SP = MP × (1 - d/100)   discount is on the marked price
Two discounts: net = a + b - ab/100
Equal SP, one +x% and one -x%: overall loss = x²/100 %
False weight: gain % = (true - false)/false × 100
SP of m articles = CP of n articles: profit % = (n - m)/m × 100
Buy x, get y free: discount = y/(x+y)
Profit p% on SP ↔ p/(100-p) × 100 % on cost
The four classic traps: adding percents instead of multiplying; using the cost as the base for a discount; using the selling price as the base for profit; taking the false weight, not the weight actually given, as the base.

5. Worked examples

a) Price rises 25%, then falls 20%. Net change?

1.25 × 0.8 = 1.00. No net change. Adding (+25 - 20) would give the trap answer of +5%.

b) Equal SP of ₹900; one article at 10% profit, the other at 10% loss. Result?

Cost of the first = 900/1.1 = 818.18; cost of the second = 900/0.9 = 1,000. Total cost 1,818.18 against 1,800 received: a loss of 18.18, which is 1%. Shortcut: x²/100 = 100/100 = 1%.

c) Cost ₹750. Marked price for 20% profit after a 10% discount?

Target SP = 750 × 1.2 = 900. SP = 0.9 × MP, so MP = 900/0.9 = ₹1,000.

d) Profit of 20% on SP. Profit on cost?

Take SP = 100, profit = 20, CP = 80. Profit on cost = 20/80 = 25%.

The four-second check (use it on every question)

  1. Base: write "base = ..." before you calculate.
  2. Ask: write what the question wants (final price? profit %? how many?).
  3. Direction: say whether the answer should be bigger or smaller than a number you know. A profit means SP above CP.
  4. Trap scan: if an option equals what you would get by adding percents or using the wrong base, that option is the bait. Re-check before you pick it.

L2 · Reading comprehension technique

Read for structure first, details second. Most RC marks are lost to options that sound right but go beyond the passage.

1. The three-minute pass

  1. Read the first and last sentence of each paragraph before the full read. That is the skeleton.
  2. As you read, label each paragraph in about five words: "usual view", "problem", "twist", "consequence".
  3. Mark contrast words: but, however, yet, although, nonetheless, whereas. The author's point usually sits after them.
  4. Mark voice words: tempting, merely, crucially, misleads, surprisingly. These carry the tone.

2. What each question type asks

TypeIt asksTest
Main ideaWhat the passage does as a wholeDoes the option cover the first and the last paragraph?
InferenceWhat must be true given the textCan you point to the sentence that forces it? "Could be" is not enough.
ToneThe author's attitude, not the people quotedLook for qualifiers: "partly", "though", "however".
Detail / EXCEPTWhat the text statesFind the line. For EXCEPT, tick the three that are stated.
FunctionWhy a sentence is thereRead the sentence before and after it.
Strengthen / weakenEffect of a new fact on a claimName the claim, then test the link between evidence and claim.

3. Five shapes of wrong option

  • Too extreme: always, never, only, all, completely, fully. Passages rarely say these.
  • Out of scope: may be true in life but the text never says it.
  • Reversal: says the opposite of the text, often with the right keywords.
  • Half-right: the first half matches the text, the second half is added.
  • True but not the answer: an accurate detail that does not answer this question.
Per question (about 45 seconds):
1. Read the stem. 2. Go to the lines it points at. 3. Predict the answer in your own words. 4. Cross out options by shape. 5. Lock.

4. Worked example

Passage

When the metro opened, planners predicted that car traffic on the parallel road would fall. It did not. Within a year the road was as congested as before, because the space freed by commuters who switched to the metro was quickly taken by drivers who had earlier avoided the route. Economists call this induced demand, and it explains why cities that build their way out of congestion by widening roads tend to find the relief temporary. The metro was not a failure: it moved far more people than the road ever could. But as a cure for road congestion alone, it disappointed.

Main idea

Answer: A new metro line did not reduce congestion on a parallel road, because freed space attracted new drivers. The passage opens with the prediction, shows why it failed, and ends on the metro's limit. An option such as "the metro was a failure" is the reversal: the author says it was not.

Inference

Answer: Widening a road is unlikely to give lasting relief. The text states this about "cities that build their way out of congestion". "Metros always fail to reduce traffic" is too extreme.

Tone

Answer: Balanced: it credits the metro for moving people while noting its limit on congestion. "Disappointed" in the last sentence is the metro's result, not the author's mood.
