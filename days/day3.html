L5 · Equations and polynomials

A complete treatment, built from first principles. By the end you should be able to derive every formula here yourself, because the exam rewards the person who can adapt a formula to a twisted question, not the one who memorised it. Roughly 80 minutes: read for 45, work the examples for 20, then Drill 5.

1. Linear equations and pairs of them

A linear equation in two unknowns, ax + by = c, is a straight line. Two such equations are two lines, and solving the pair means finding where the lines meet. That picture gives the three possible outcomes without any algebra.

CoefficientsLinesSolutions
a1/a2 ≠ b1/b2Cross at one pointExactly one
a1/a2 = b1/b2 ≠ c1/c2Parallel, distinctNone
a1/a2 = b1/b2 = c1/c2The same lineInfinitely many
Two-step test for "no solution": first make the coefficients proportional (this gives candidates for k), then check the constants for each candidate. A candidate that also makes the constants proportional gives infinitely many solutions, not none. Drill 5 has exactly this question.

Solving: elimination (make one coefficient equal, subtract) is faster than substitution unless one equation already reads x = ... For word problems, define each unknown with its unit, write one equation per fact, and check that the number of equations equals the number of unknowns.

2. The quadratic from first principles

Start from ax² + bx + c = 0 (a ≠ 0). Divide by a and complete the square:

x² + (b/a)x + c/a = 0
(x + b/2a)² = b²/4a² − c/a = (b² − 4ac)/4a²
x = [−b ± √(b² − 4ac)] / 2a

The expression under the root, D = b² − 4ac, decides everything: a square of a real number cannot be negative.

DRootsGraph y = ax² + bx + c
D > 0Two distinct real rootsCuts the x-axis twice
D = 0Two equal real roots, x = −b/2aTouches the x-axis
D < 0Two complex conjugate rootsNever meets the x-axis
D a perfect square (integer a, b, c)Rational rootsFactorises over the integers

The roots are symmetric about x = −b/2a, the axis of the parabola. That single fact gives the vertex, the sum of roots, and the extreme value (section 6).

3. Vieta: roots without finding them

If α and β are the roots, then ax² + bx + c = a(x − α)(x − β) = a[x² − (α + β)x + αβ]. Comparing coefficients:

α + β = −b/a
αβ = c/a

Any symmetric expression in the roots (unchanged when α and β swap) can be written using only S = α + β and P = αβ. That is why you never need to find the roots.

α² + β²S² − 2P

Derivation of the cube line: S³ = (α + β)³ = α³ + β³ + 3αβ(α + β), so α³ + β³ = S³ − 3PS. Every higher power follows the same pattern: build from lower ones.

4. Building an equation from new roots

Any quadratic with roots r1, r2 is x² − (r1 + r2)x + r1r2 = 0. For transformed roots, either compute the new sum and product from S and P, or substitute directly.

Trap: the equation is x² − (sum)x + product. The sign on the middle term is minus the sum. A sum of 28 gives −28x.

Derivation of the ratio line. Let the roots be m·t and n·t. Then (m + n)t = −b/a and mn·t² = c/a. Square the first and divide by the second to remove t: (m + n)²/(mn) = b²/(ac), which rearranges to mn b² = (m + n)² ac. With m = 2, n = 3 and the equation 2x² − 5x + k = 0: 6(25) = 25(2)(k), so k = 3.

Common root. If two quadratics share a root r, then r satisfies both, so it satisfies their difference (a linear equation). Subtract, solve for r, substitute back. If the quadratics factorise, the shortcut is to list the roots of one and test.

6. The parabola: extremes, ranges and signs

Complete the square: ax² + bx + c = a(x + b/2a)² + (4ac − b²)/4a. A square is never negative, so:

a > 0: minimum value = −D/4a, at x = −b/2a
a < 0: maximum value = −D/4a, at x = −b/2a
Positive for every real x ⇔ a > 0 and D < 0
Negative for every real x ⇔ a < 0 and D < 0

Two numbers with a fixed sum have the largest product when equal (the vertex halfway between the roots). Two numbers with a fixed product have the smallest sum when equal (this is AM–GM, in L6).

7. Equations that look different but reduce to quadratics

  • Substitution. If an expression repeats, name it. x⁴ − 5x² + 4 = 0 with t = x² becomes t² − 5t + 4 = 0. For x + 1/x = t, you get x² + 1/x² = t² − 2 and x³ + 1/x³ = t³ − 3t.
  • Radical equations. Isolate one root, square, solve, then test every answer in the original. Squaring can create roots that were never there. A principal square root is never negative, so check the sign of the other side first (√(2x + 3) = x forces x ≥ 0).
  • Fractional equations. Note the values that make a denominator zero before you start, and reject them at the end.
  • Exponential equations such as 4x − 5·2x + 4 = 0: set t = 2x (so 4x = t²), solve for t, and reject t ≤ 0, since 2x is always positive.

Complex roots in one paragraph

Define i² = −1. When D < 0 the roots are p ± qi. For a polynomial with real coefficients, complex roots come in conjugate pairs, so a cubic with real coefficients always has at least one real root. Vieta's formulas hold unchanged: for x² + 2x + 5 = 0, S = −2, P = 5, and α² + β² = S² − 2P = −6, even though neither root is real.

8. Polynomials

A polynomial of degree n has at most n roots. Dividing p(x) by d(x) gives p(x) = d(x)q(x) + r(x), where the degree of r is less than the degree of d.

Remainder theorem: the remainder when p(x) is divided by (x − a) is p(a)
Factor theorem: (x − a) divides p(x) ⇔ p(a) = 0

Proof of the remainder theorem. Divide by (x − a): p(x) = (x − a)q(x) + r, where r is a constant (its degree is less than 1). Put x = a: p(a) = 0 + r. The factor theorem is the case r = 0.

  • Division by (ax + b): the remainder is p(−b/a).
  • Division by a quadratic: the remainder has degree at most 1, so write p(x) = (x − r1)(x − r2)q(x) + (mx + n), then plug in each root of the divisor to get two equations for m and n.
  • Hunting for a rational root: if p/q (lowest terms) is a root of an integer polynomial, p divides the constant term and q divides the leading coefficient. For x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6, test ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6: p(1) = 0 gives a root.

Cubic Vieta. Expanding (x − α)(x − β)(x − γ) and comparing with ax³ + bx² + cx + d:

α + β + γ = −b/a
αβ + βγ + γα = c/a
αβγ = −d/a
α² + β² + γ² = (sum)² − 2(sum of pairs)

Useful identity: a³ + b³ + c³ − 3abc = (a + b + c)(a² + b² + c² − ab − bc − ca). If a + b + c = 0, then a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc.

9. Turning words into equations

  • Consecutive numbers: n, n + 1, n + 2; consecutive evens: n, n + 2.
  • Digits: a two-digit number is 10a + b; reversing gives 10b + a; the difference is 9(a − b).
  • Speed problems: time = distance/speed, so two journeys of the same distance give an equation in the speeds. With a current, speeds add or subtract.
  • Fractions of a quantity: "a third of x" is x/3. Say aloud what each term represents before writing the equation.
  • The answer must be sensible. Reject negative lengths, non-integer people, and roots that violate a condition stated in the problem.

10. Traps that cost marks

  • Writing x² + (sum)x + product when forming an equation: the middle sign is minus the sum.
  • Using D < 0 when the question says "real roots" (it needs D ≥ 0), or D ≤ 0 for "positive for all x" (it needs D < 0).
  • Forgetting that "values of k" can have two answers and the question may want the sum, product or count.
  • Not testing roots after squaring.
  • Treating "no solution" and "infinitely many solutions" as the same case: they differ in the constant term.
  • Applying the factor theorem with the wrong sign: (x + 1) divides p(x) means p(−1) = 0, not p(1).

11. Worked examples

a) If α, β are roots of 3x² − 5x + 1 = 0, find α³ + β³.

S = 5/3, P = 1/3. S³ − 3PS = 125/27 − 3(1/3)(5/3) = 125/27 − 45/27 = 80/27.

b) For which k does x² − 2(k + 1)x + k² + 5 = 0 have real roots?

D/4 = (k + 1)² − (k² + 5) = 2k − 4 ≥ 0, so k ≥ 2. The k² terms cancel and the condition becomes linear: check by expanding rather than assuming a quadratic condition.

c) The roots of x² − 9x + k = 0 differ by 3. Find k.

(α − β)² = S² − 4P: 9 = 81 − 4k, so k = 18. The roots are 6 and 3.

d) Find the minimum of x² + 4x + 7 and the range of 1/(x² + 4x + 7).

x² + 4x + 7 = (x + 2)² + 3, minimum 3 at x = −2. The reciprocal of a number that is at least 3 lies in (0, 1/3], so the range is 0 < y ≤ 1/3.

e) p(x) leaves remainder 5 on division by (x − 1) and 8 on division by (x − 2). Find the remainder on division by (x − 1)(x − 2).

The remainder is mx + n. p(1) = m + n = 5 and p(2) = 2m + n = 8, so m = 3, n = 2. The remainder is 3x + 2.

f) Solve 4x − 5·2x + 4 = 0.

t = 2x: t² − 5t + 4 = 0, so t = 1 or 4, giving x = 0 or 2. Both t values are positive so both are accepted.

g) Solve √(x + 7) − √(x − 1) = 2.

Move: √(x + 7) = 2 + √(x − 1). Square: x + 7 = 4 + 4√(x − 1) + x − 1, so 4 = 4√(x − 1), √(x − 1) = 1 and x = 2. Test: √9 − √1 = 3 − 1 = 2. Valid.

h) A two-digit number is 4 times the sum of its digits, and reversing the digits gives a number 27 more than the original. Find it.

10a + b = 4(a + b) gives 6a = 3b, so b = 2a. Reversed minus original = 9(b − a) = 27, so b − a = 3, and with b = 2a: a = 3, b = 6. The number is 36 (check: 36 = 4 × 9; 63 − 36 = 27).

The four-second check

  1. What is asked: the roots, a sum of roots, a parameter, a count, or an extreme value?
  2. Choose the tool: Vieta for symmetric expressions; discriminant for conditions on k; vertex for extremes; factor theorem for divisibility.
  3. Direction check: does the answer satisfy the original equation or condition? Substitute a simple value.
  4. Trap scan: if an option is the result of a sign slip or a lost factor of 2, it is bait.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Symmetric-root and discriminant questions: 40 seconds. Do these first.
  2. Questions that need two theorems chained (factor then remainder) can take 80 seconds. Skip if the setup is not clear at 25 seconds.
  3. For options that are intervals, test one number from each region instead of solving fully.
  4. A wrong answer costs 1 and a blank 0. Cut at least one option by a direction check before guessing.

L6 · Inequalities and modulus

Inequalities behave like equations with one dangerous exception: multiplying or dividing by a negative reverses the sign. Modulus is distance, and once you read it that way, most "cases" work disappears. Roughly 80 minutes: read for 45, examples for 20, then Drill 6.

1. The rules, and why each holds

  • Add or subtract the same number on both sides: the direction is unchanged (a shift along the number line moves both sides equally).
  • Multiply or divide by a positive number: unchanged.
  • Multiply or divide by a negative number: reverse the sign. Reason: −1 reflects the number line, so 2 < 5 becomes −2 > −5.
  • Never multiply by an expression whose sign you do not know. For x/(x − 2) > 1, do not clear the denominator; move everything to one side and use a sign chart.
  • Taking reciprocals of positive numbers reverses the order (2 < 3 but 1/2 > 1/3); for numbers of opposite signs, it keeps it (−2 < 3 and 1/(−2) < 1/3).
  • Squaring keeps the order only for non-negative numbers. −5 < 3 but 25 > 9. Use |a| < |b| ⇔ a² < b².
  • Adding two inequalities of the same direction is allowed; subtracting them is not.

Solutions are intervals. Write them with the endpoints marked: (a, b) excludes both, [a, b] includes both. A strict inequality (<, >) excludes the endpoint; a non-strict one includes it. At a point where a denominator is zero, the endpoint is always excluded.

2. Quadratic inequalities: the sign chart

The graph of y = ax² + bx + c with a > 0 is a U. It is below the axis between the roots and above it outside them.

a > 0, roots r1 < r2:
(x − r1)(x − r2) < 0 ⇔ r1 < x < r2
(x − r1)(x − r2) > 0 ⇔ x < r1 or x > r2

Method for any product or quotient of factors (the wavy-curve method):

  1. Move everything to one side so the other is 0. Factorise fully.
  2. Mark each root (zero of a factor) on the number line, in order.
  3. Start from the far right: the expression has the sign of the product of the leading coefficients (positive if all factors look like (x − r)).
  4. Move left. The sign flips at every root of odd multiplicity and stays the same across a root of even multiplicity (the factor is squared, so it does not change sign).
  5. Collect the regions with the wanted sign. Add back roots with even multiplicity if the inequality is ≤ 0 (they make the expression zero), and exclude roots from the denominator always.

Solve (x − 1)(x + 2)²(x − 4) ≤ 0.

Roots: −2 (even), 1, 4. Right of 4 the sign is +. Cross 4: −. Cross 1: +. At −2 the sign does not change: +. So the expression is negative on (1, 4) and zero at x = 1, 4, −2. Answer: 1 ≤ x ≤ 4 or x = −2. The isolated point −2 is the part most students miss.

Trap: x² < 4 is −2 < x < 2, not x < 2. And x² > 4 is x < −2 or x > 2, which is not a single interval.

3. Rational inequalities

For (x − 3)/(x + 1) ≥ 0, the sign of a quotient is the sign of the product, but x = −1 is forbidden. Roots: 3 (numerator, allowed if the sign is ≥) and −1 (denominator, never allowed). Right of 3: +; between −1 and 3: −; left of −1: +. So x < −1 or x ≥ 3.

Do not cross-multiply. To solve 2/(x − 1) > 1, write 2/(x − 1) − 1 > 0, i.e. (3 − x)/(x − 1) > 0, i.e. (x − 3)/(x − 1) < 0, so 1 < x < 3. Cross-multiplying would have required you to know the sign of x − 1 first.

4. Modulus is distance

|x| = x if x ≥ 0, and −x if x < 0
|x − a| = distance from x to a on the number line
|a − b| = |b − a|, |ab| = |a||b|, |a/b| = |a|/|b|

With the distance reading, the standard forms need no cases:

StatementMeaningSolution
|x − a| = r (r > 0)Distance exactly r from ax = a − r or a + r
|x − a| < rWithin r of aa − r < x < a + r
|x − a| > rMore than r from ax < a − r or x > a + r
|x| < negative numberImpossibleNo solution
|x| > negative numberAlways trueAll real x
|x − a| = |x − b|Equidistant from a and bx = (a + b)/2

Triangle inequality: |a + b| ≤ |a| + |b|, with equality when a and b have the same sign (or one is zero). Also |a − b| ≥ ||a| − |b||.

5. Sums of moduli: the minimum is at a median point

|x − a| + |x − b| is the total distance from x to two points. For a < b it equals b − a for every x between them, and is larger outside. So the minimum value is b − a.

For three or more points, the sum |x − a1| + ... + |x − an| is smallest at the median of the points. With points 1, 4, 9, it is smallest at x = 4: 3 + 0 + 5 = 8. The function is a chain of straight segments with kinks at the points, so check the value at each kink.

Minimum of |x − 2| + |x − 5| + |x − 11|?

Median is 5: 3 + 0 + 6 = 9.

6. Equations with modulus: cases

When the distance trick does not fit (several moduli with different coefficients), split the number line at the zeros of each modulus and solve in each region, keeping only answers that lie in their region.

Solve |x − 1| + |x − 4| = 7.

Zeros at 1 and 4. Region x < 1: (1 − x) + (4 − x) = 5 − 2x = 7, x = −1 (valid). Region 1 ≤ x ≤ 4: the sum is 3, not 7. Region x > 4: 2x − 5 = 7, x = 6 (valid). Answer: x = −1, 6.

Nested modulus, e.g. ||x − 2| − 3| = 1: peel from the outside. |x − 2| − 3 = 1 or −1, so |x − 2| = 4 or 2, giving x = 6, −2, 4, 0. Count the answers: four. Each inner value must be non-negative; here 4 and 2 are.

Squaring is safe for |A| = |B| (A² = B²) and |A| < |B| (A² < B²) because both sides are non-negative. It is not safe if one side may be negative: |x| = x − 3 requires x − 3 ≥ 0 first.

7. Maximum and minimum: AM-GM and friends

For a, b ≥ 0: (a + b)/2 ≥ √(ab), with equality only when a = b
For n positive numbers: arithmetic mean ≥ geometric mean
x + 1/x ≥ 2 for x > 0, and ≤ −2 for x < 0

Proof for two numbers: (√a − √b)² ≥ 0 gives a + b − 2√(ab) ≥ 0. Equality needs √a = √b.

How to use it. If the sum is fixed, the product is largest when the terms are equal. If the product is fixed, the sum is smallest when the terms are equal. When the sum is not fixed, split terms so that the variable parts cancel: to maximise x(10 − x) the sum is already 10; to minimise 3x + 12/x use the fact that the product is 36 (fixed), so the minimum is 2√36 = 12 when 3x = 12/x, i.e. x = 2.

Trap: AM-GM needs positive terms and an achievable equality. If the equality point is outside the allowed range of x, the minimum is at the boundary instead.

Other useful facts: a² + b² ≥ 2ab; (a + b)(1/a + 1/b) ≥ 4 for positive a, b; a³ + b³ ≥ ab(a + b) for positive a, b.

8. Traps that cost marks

  • Forgetting to reverse the sign on division by a negative (often hidden in −x > 3).
  • Cross-multiplying a rational inequality.
  • Including the denominator's root, or omitting a squared factor's root when the sign is ≤ 0.
  • Reading |x − 3| < 2 as x < 5 only. It is 1 < x < 5.
  • Squaring both sides when one side can be negative.
  • Counting integer solutions: include or exclude endpoints carefully, and recount after deciding the interval.
  • Using AM-GM when the terms can be negative.

9. Worked examples

a) How many integers satisfy x² − 5x − 14 < 0?

(x − 7)(x + 2) < 0 gives −2 < x < 7. Integers: −1 to 6, which is 8 values.

b) Solve (2x − 1)/(x + 3) ≤ 1.

(2x − 1)/(x + 3) − 1 = (x − 4)/(x + 3) ≤ 0, so −3 < x ≤ 4.

c) Solve |2x − 3| ≤ 7.

−7 ≤ 2x − 3 ≤ 7, so −4 ≤ 2x ≤ 10, so −2 ≤ x ≤ 5.

d) Solve |x + 2| > 2x − 1.

If 2x − 1 < 0 (x < 1/2) it holds automatically since the left side is non-negative. If x ≥ 1/2 we need x + 2 > 2x − 1 (so x < 3) or x + 2 < −(2x − 1) (x < −1/3, which contradicts x ≥ 1/2). So 1/2 ≤ x < 3. Together: x < 3.

e) Minimum of x + 16/x for x > 0?

The product of the terms is 16 (fixed), so the minimum is 2√16 = 8, at x = 4.

f) If a + b = 12 with a, b > 0, the maximum of ab?

Fixed sum, so the maximum is at a = b = 6: 36.

g) For how many integers x is |x − 1| + |x − 6| < 8?

Between 1 and 6 the sum is 5 (always < 8). Outside, e.g. x > 6: 2x − 7 < 8, x < 7.5. For x < 1: 7 − 2x < 8, x > −0.5. So −0.5 < x < 7.5, integers 0 to 7: 8 values.

The four-second check

  1. One side zero? Move everything across before you do anything else.
  2. Draw the line with every root marked, and say which are open and which are closed.
  3. Test a point in each region, usually 0 if it is not a root.
  4. Endpoint scan: denominator roots out, squared-factor roots in (for ≤ 0), strictness as stated.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Single-modulus and single-quadratic questions: 40 seconds with the distance reading and a quick sign test.
  2. For "how many integers", find the interval, then count: last minus first plus one.
  3. If you can test the options, plug in one number from each option interval (0, an endpoint, a large value) rather than solving fully.
  4. Skip anything that needs three-region case analysis at 30 seconds without a plan.

L7 · Simple and compound interest, and growth

Simple interest is a straight line; compound interest is repeated percentage change. If you are comfortable with successive percentages from Day 1, this is the same idea with a calendar attached. About 25 minutes.

1. Simple interest

SI = P × R × T / 100
Amount A = P + SI = P(1 + RT/100)

The interest is the same every year, because it is always charged on the original principal P. R is the rate per year in per cent and T is in years. Convert months to years (9 months = 3/4 year) before using the formula.

  • Doubling: money doubles when SI = P, so RT = 100. At 8% it takes 12.5 years.
  • Finding the rate or time from two amounts: the difference between two amounts is the interest for the gap in time. A = 5,800 after 3 years and 6,400 after 5 years gives 600 for 2 years, 300 per year, so P = 5,800 − 900 = 4,900.
  • Split investments: if x is invested at r1 and (S − x) at r2, the total interest is a weighted average problem: the overall rate lies between r1 and r2 and the split follows alligation from L3.

2. Compound interest

Each period, interest is added to the principal, so the next period is charged on the larger sum. It is successive percentage increase: multiply by (1 + r) each period.

A = P(1 + R/100)n
CI = A − P

If interest compounds half-yearly, use rate R/2 and 2n periods; quarterly, R/4 and 4n periods. Always check that the rate and the period match.

QuantityCompound interestSimple interest
Year 1PR/100PR/100
Year 2Larger: interest on interestSame as year 1
CI − SI for 2 yearsP(R/100)²
CI − SI for 3 yearsP(R/100)²(3 + R/100)

Why CI − SI for 2 years is P(R/100)². CI = P[(1 + r)² − 1] = P(2r + r²), and SI = 2Pr, so the difference is Pr². That is the interest earned in year 2 on the year-1 interest. At 10% on 5,000 the difference is 5,000 × 0.01 = 50.

Trap: the formula A = P(1 + R/100)n gives the amount. The interest is A − P. Questions that ask for "interest" and give A as an option are baiting you.

Fast arithmetic. Do successive multiplication rather than powers when n is 2 or 3: 8,000 at 10% for 2 years is 8,000 × 1.1 × 1.1 = 9,680. A 20% rate is a factor of 6/5 each year, which suits denominations that divide by 5. A 12.5% rate is 9/8, and so on: choose P as a multiple of the denominators.

3. Growth and depreciation

Population, a bacterial culture, a machine losing value: all are compound interest with a different name. Growth of r per cent per period multiplies by (1 + r/100) each period; depreciation by (1 − r/100).

Value after n periods = V0(1 ± r/100)n
Different rates each year: multiply the factors in order

The rule of 72 says money doubles in about 72/R years at compound rate R%. It is an estimate for rates in the 6–10% range, not an exact value.

Same percentage up and down. Increase by x% then decrease by x%: the net change is −x²/100 per cent. 20% up then 20% down is −4%.

A town of 50,000 grows 10% in year 1, then falls 10% in year 2. Population after two years?

50,000 × 1.1 × 0.9 = 49,500 (net −1%, since x²/100 = 1).

A sum triples in 3 years at compound interest. In how many years does it become 27 times?

Each 3 years multiplies by 3, so 27 = 3³ takes 9 years.

4. Traps and shortcuts

  • Mixing the periods: half-yearly compounding needs both the rate halved and the periods doubled.
  • Giving the amount when the question asks for interest.
  • Using SI for the first year and CI for later years: for a fractional year the standard treatment is CI for the whole years, then SI on that amount for the fraction.
  • Instalment problems: each instalment pays off a present value; work backward from the last payment, dividing by (1 + r) each step.
  • If an option is exactly the SI answer, it is a trap for CI questions, and vice versa.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Decide simple or compound, annual or not, and write the formula before touching numbers.
  2. Choose the cheapest arithmetic: successive factors, not powers of 1.0x.
  3. A direction check helps: CI > SI for more than one period at the same rate.

L8 · Time and work

Time and work is speed problems in disguise: work is the distance, a worker's rate is the speed. Convert everything to a rate and rates simply add. About 25 minutes.

1. The one idea: rate = work / time

Work = rate × time
If A finishes a job in a days, A's rate is 1/a of the job per day
Working together: rates add

If A takes 10 days and B takes 15, together they do 1/10 + 1/15 = 1/6 per day, so they take 6 days. For two people the shortcut is product over sum: (a × b)/(a + b) = 150/25 = 6.

It is faster to choose the work as the LCM of the times. Take the job as 30 units: A does 3 a day, B does 2 a day, together 5 a day, 6 days. Whole-number rates remove the fractions.

2. Variations that appear in tests

  • Leaving and joining: work in stages. A and B work 3 days, then A leaves. With 30 units, A+B do 15 in 3 days; B does the remaining 15 at 2 a day, 7.5 more days.
  • Alternate days: compute the work in a two-day cycle, find how many full cycles fit, then handle the leftover carefully (it may finish partway through a day).
  • A negative worker (a leak or an outlet) has a negative rate. A pipe filling a tank in 6 hours and a leak emptying it in 12 give 1/6 − 1/12 = 1/12 per hour, so 12 hours.
  • Efficiency: if A is 50% more efficient than B, their rates are in the ratio 3 : 2 and their times are in the ratio 2 : 3.
  • Wages: pay is split in the ratio of work done (rate × days), not of days present.
  • Men, days, hours: M1D1H1/W1 = M2D2H2/W2, assuming equal efficiency. More men means fewer days: it is an inverse relationship.

A can do a job in 12 days and B in 18. They work together for 4 days, then A leaves. How many more days does B need?

Take 36 units. A does 3 a day, B does 2 a day. Together in 4 days: 20. Remaining 16 at 2 a day: 8 more days.

Pipe P fills a tank in 20 minutes, Q in 30 minutes and an outlet empties it in 60. All open together, how long to fill?

Tank = 60 units. P: +3, Q: +2, outlet: −1. Net 4 per minute, so 15 minutes.

3. Traps

  • Averaging times: two workers taking 10 and 15 days do not take 12.5 days together; rates add, times do not.
  • Forgetting that a worker who leaves "after x days" has already contributed for those days.
  • Mixing hours and days, or counting days where the work is split across a cycle.
  • Using "men and days" proportionality when the efficiencies differ.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Pick the work as the LCM first. If the first two times are 12 and 18, say 36.
  2. Write each rate as a whole number, then add them.
  3. Direction check: the combined time must be shorter than the fastest worker alone.

L9 · Para-jumbles

A para-jumble is a logic puzzle with words. Do not read the options first and do not try to "feel" the order. Find the opening sentence, then chain the rest by the links between sentences. About 35 minutes including the drill.

1. The method

  1. Find the opener. It introduces the subject without needing anything before it: a full name, a general statement, a definition, a date. It must not begin with a pronoun, "this", "such", "however", "but", "thus" or "also".
  2. Find the closer. It often concludes, gives a consequence, a final example, or looks ahead.
  3. Find mandatory pairs. Two sentences that must sit together (a question and its answer, a pronoun and its noun, "first" and "second", a quote and its response).
  4. Use the options to test. Cross out any option that breaks an opener or a mandatory pair. Usually two checks leave one option.
  5. Read your order once to confirm it flows.

2. The cues

CueWhat it tells you
Pronouns (he, she, it, they, their)The noun they replace must come earlier. A sentence starting with a pronoun is never the opener.
"This", "such", "these"Refers to an idea in the sentence just before. Look for that idea.
"The" with a nounIf "the reason" or "the practice" is mentioned, the thing must have been introduced as "a" or described earlier.
Connectors: however, yet, butContrasts the sentence before. It cannot open.
Connectors: therefore, thus, soResult of a cause just given.
Connectors: for example, for instanceFollows the general claim it illustrates.
Dates and sequence wordsChronology: earlier first. "Eventually" and "finally" come late.
A questionThe next sentence usually answers it.
Trap: a sentence that looks like a good opener because it is a statement about the topic may still depend on something earlier through a word like "also" or "another". Read for the first word that points backward.

3. Worked example

P. Its roots can reach a hundred metres into the earth.
Q. The mesquite is a desert tree with an unusual strategy for finding water.
R. This lets it draw on groundwater long after the surface has dried.
S. Unlike most trees, it invests more in what lies below the ground than above it.

Q opens: it introduces the tree with "a" and states a puzzle. S follows, since "it" and "unlike most trees" elaborate the strategy. P gives the detail ("its roots"), and R's "This" refers to the hundred-metre roots. Order: QSPR. Check: P cannot open ("Its"), R cannot open ("This").

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Spend 20 seconds on the opener. If two sentences qualify, test each against the mandatory pairs.
  2. With four sentences, look for a pair, then eliminate options. Do not permute all 24 orders.
  3. If you cannot find a pair by 40 seconds, skip. A wrong answer costs a mark.
