L3 · Ratio, proportion, partnership and mixtures

Four topics, one idea. A ratio splits a whole into shares. Partnership splits profit in the ratio of money × time. A mixture is a weighted average whose weights are the quantities. In every question, find what one share is worth, and everything else is arithmetic.

1. What a ratio is, and what it is not

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind by division. 3 : 5 means "for every 3 of the first there are 5 of the second". The units cancel, and the order matters: 3 : 5 and 5 : 3 are different statements.

A ratio is a part-to-part comparison. The part-to-whole fractions come from adding the shares.

Trap: "boys : girls = 3 : 5" does not mean boys are 3/5 of the class. They are 3/8 of the class and 3/5 of the girls. Always decide which base the question uses.

2. The k-method: your default engine

If two numbers are in the ratio a : b, they are ak and bk for some multiplier k. The ratio gives the shape; one hard fact (a sum, a difference, a product, an LCM, an age change) gives the size. Write the shapes, plug in the one hard fact, solve for k.

Sum S: k = S/(a+b)
Difference D: k = D/(b-a)
Product P: ab k² = P
Sum of squares: (a² + b²) k² = value
Coprime a, b: HCF = k and LCM = abk

Why the HCF/LCM line holds: if a and b share no factor, the largest number that divides both ak and bk is exactly k, and the smallest number both divide is abk. This turns a "LCM is 1,155, ratio 7 : 11" question into 77k = 1,155 in one line.

Check yourself: two numbers in the ratio 4 : 7 have difference 36. The sum?

k = 36/3 = 12. Numbers 48 and 84, sum 132. A shortcut: sum/difference = 11/3, so sum = 36 × 11/3 = 132.

3. Linking ratios

Given a : b and b : c, the ratio a : b : c needs the common term b to have the same value in both. Scale each ratio so the b-terms match, using the LCM of the b-parts.

a : b = 2 : 3 and b : c = 4 : 5
LCM of 3 and 4 = 12
a : b = 8 : 12, b : c = 12 : 15
so a : b : c = 8 : 12 : 15

For a chain you only need the end terms: a/d = (a/b)(b/c)(c/d), because every middle term cancels. For 2 : 3, 4 : 5, 6 : 7 this gives 48/105 = 16/35 with no LCM at all.

4. Changing a ratio

Three situations, three invariants. Name what stays fixed before you write an equation.

SituationWhat stays fixedEquation

Adding the same positive number to both terms always moves the ratio towards 1 : 1. Use this as a direction check: 3 : 5 becoming 3 : 4 is a move towards 1 : 1, so an addition is plausible, and a subtraction would have moved it away.

A and B hold money in the ratio 5 : 3. After A gives 20 to B, the ratio is 3 : 5. How much did each have?

The total is fixed at 8 shares. A went from 5 shares to 3 shares, so 2 shares = 20 and one share = 10. Before: A 50, B 30. After: A 30, B 50. Check: 30 : 50 = 3 : 5.

5. Componendo and dividendo

If a/b = c/d, then adding 1 to both sides gives (a+b)/b = (c+d)/d, and subtracting 1 gives (a−b)/b = (c−d)/d. Dividing the first by the second, the b and d cancel:

a/b = c/d → (a + b)/(a − b) = (c + d)/(c − d)

It works in both directions. If (a+b)/(a−b) = 7/3, then a/b = (7+3)/(7−3) = 5/2. Use it whenever the question gives a ratio of sums and differences and asks for the original ratio.

Equal ratios: if a/b = c/d = e/f = r, then (a + c + e)/(b + d + f) = r too. The same ratio holds for the sums.

7. Two ratios, two multipliers

When two different ratios are given about the same people (incomes and expenditures, boys and girls in two classes), each ratio gets its own multiplier. One multiplier for both is the most common wrong turn in this topic. Two unknowns need two facts, and the question always supplies them (equal savings, equal totals).

Incomes of A and B are in the ratio 3 : 2, expenditures in the ratio 2 : 1, and each saves 1,000. Find A's income.

Incomes 3x and 2x; expenditures 2y and y. Savings: 3x − 2y = 1,000 and 2x − y = 1,000. From the second, y = 2x − 1,000. Substituting: 3x − 4x + 2,000 = 1,000, so x = 1,000 and A's income is 3,000. Check: A spends 2,000 and saves 1,000; B earns 2,000, spends 1,000, saves 1,000.

8. Partnership: the law of rupee-months

Profit is the reward for money kept in the business for time. Every rupee that stays one month earns the same amount. So the profit is split in the ratio of capital × months.

Profit share ∝ (capital × months)
1. Write each partner's months in the business
2. Multiply capital by months for each period and add
3. Reduce the ratio
4. Divide the profit by the total parts
WordingMonths in the business (12-month year)
Starts the business12
Joins after 4 months12 − 4 = 8
Joins 3 months before the end3
Leaves after 9 months9
Withdraws half after 6 monthsfull capital 6, half capital 6

Salary and commission come out first. A working partner's salary or commission is paid from the profit before the rest is shared by capital × time. A sleeping partner just gets his share of the ratio.

Changing capital: split the year into periods in which the capital is constant, compute capital × months for each period, and add them. Never average the capital.

Traps: using the capital ratio alone; counting months from the wrong end ("joins after 4 months" is 8 months in, not 4); sharing the profit before taking out the salary; assuming everyone's months are 12.

9. Mixtures: weighted averages and alligation

Mix quantities q1 and q2 of two things with values v1 and v2 (prices, concentrations, speeds). The mean is the weighted average m = (q1v1 + q2v2)/(q1 + q2). Rearranging: q1(m − v1) = q2(v2 − m), which gives

q1 : q2 = (v2 − m) : (m − v1)
cheaper : dearer = (dearer − mean) : (mean − cheaper)

That is alligation. Read it as "the quantities are in inverse proportion to the distances from the mean": the closer the mean is to one ingredient, the more of that ingredient there is. Use this sentence to catch a reversed cross: if the mean is nearer the dearer item, the dearer item must be the larger quantity.

  • The mean is a cost price, not a selling price. If a mixture is sold at S with a profit of p%, the mean is S/(1 + p/100).
  • It works on any "per unit" figure: price per kg, fraction of milk per litre, speed. For mixtures given as ratios, convert each to the fraction of one ingredient first (3 : 2 milk to water is 3/5 milk).
  • Only two ingredients at a time. For three, find the combined mean first, as in the tea example.

Dilution: adding water changes the total but not the amount of the other ingredient. Write "the stuff that does not change" first: new concentration × new total = old concentration × old total.

Replacement: from a vessel of V litres, remove x litres of the mixture and replace with water. The original ingredient is reduced by the factor (1 − x/V). Repeating n times:

Original left = V × (1 − x/V)n
Left : total = (1 − x/V)n

Derivation: each time, the fraction x/V of whatever is in the vessel is removed, so the ingredient left is multiplied by (1 − x/V). To adjust a ratio, use the component: removing x litres of a mixture that is a fraction f acid removes f × x of acid.

Traps: reversing the cross; using the selling price as the mean; adding ratios of two different mixtures directly (convert to fractions first); treating removed liquid as pure; stopping after one replacement.

10. Worked examples

a) Two numbers are in the ratio 5 : 8. If each is increased by 10, the ratio becomes 7 : 10. Find the numbers.

(5k + 10)/(8k + 10) = 7/10 → 50k + 100 = 56k + 70 → k = 5. The numbers are 25 and 40. Check: 35/50 = 7/10.

b) A, B, C invest 40,000, 60,000 and 80,000. B joins 3 months late and C leaves 3 months early. Profit 43,500. A's share?

A 12 months, B 9, C 9. Capital × months (in thousands): 40 × 12 = 480; 60 × 9 = 540; 80 × 9 = 720. The ratio 480 : 540 : 720 reduces to 8 : 9 : 12, which is 29 parts. 43,500/29 = 1,500 a part, so A gets 8 × 1,500 = 12,000. A clean answer like this is a good sign: exam numbers are chosen to divide.

c) Sugar at 36 per kg is mixed with sugar at 45 per kg. At what price per kg must the mixture be sold for a 10% profit, if the two are mixed in the ratio 2 : 1?

Cost = (2 × 36 + 45)/3 = 39. SP = 39 × 1.1 = 42.90. Note that you did not need alligation: the ratio was given, so a direct weighted average is enough. Choose the tool the question needs.

d) A 60-litre vessel of pure milk has 12 litres drawn off and replaced with water. The step is done a second time. How much milk is in the final mixture?

60 × (1 − 12/60)² = 60 × 0.64 = 38.4 litres. Water is 21.6 litres. The shortcut and the step-by-step agree: 60 → 48 → 38.4.

e) Two vessels hold milk and water in the ratios 5 : 1 and 1 : 2. They are mixed in the ratio 2 : 1. What is the milk fraction of the result?

Milk fractions: 5/6 and 1/3. Weighted average with weights 2 and 1: (2 × 5/6 + 1 × 1/3)/3 = (5/3 + 1/3)/3 = 2/3. The result is 2/3 milk, a milk to water ratio of 2 : 1.

f) Partners A and B invest 20,000 and 30,000. After 6 months A adds 10,000 and B withdraws 10,000. Annual profit 33,000. Shares?

A: 20 × 6 + 30 × 6 = 300. B: 30 × 6 + 20 × 6 = 300. Equal. Each gets 16,500. The changes cancel because A adds what B removes.

Before you commit (4 seconds)

  1. Fix the share: write "one share = ..." or "k = ...". Name what the share is of.
  2. Name the invariant: sum, difference, ratio of time, or the amount of the unchanged ingredient.
  3. Tool check: ratio problems use k; partnership uses capital × time; mixtures use a weighted average or alligation.
  4. Direction check: is the answer between the two ingredient values (a mean must be)? Is the larger share going to the larger investor-time? If not, you reversed something.

Under the clock: 60 seconds a question

  1. Two-line questions with one ratio and one hard fact: 40 seconds. Do these first.
  2. Partnership with 3 phases, or mixtures with 3 stages, can take 90 seconds. If you have not set up the equations by 30 seconds, skip.
  3. Trap options are built from the wrong base. If an option equals what the shortcut-wrong method gives, slow down.
  4. A wrong answer costs 1, a blank costs 0. Cut at least one option by direction check before you guess.

L4 · Linear arrangements

A linear-arrangement set is a jigsaw with a fixed number of slots. Fill the strongest clues first, keep only what is forced, and branch only when you must. Done in order, a set takes about four minutes.

1. The 60-second setup

  1. Draw the slots. Write the seat numbers 1 to n left to right on your sheet, with a short underline for each. Never solve in your head.
  2. List the people in a column on the right so you can tick them off.
  3. Check the orientation. If everyone faces north, left and right are the same as on the page. Decide this before reading a single clue.
  4. Rewrite each clue in shorthand (table below), then read the question stems before you solve: they tell you what to look for.

2. Left and right: the orientation rule

"Left" and "right" in a clue belong to the person described, not to you. Everyone facing north means their left is the left of the page. If someone faces south, their left is the right of the page. Redraw each clue in page directions before placing it.

Facing north: own left = page left
Facing south: own left = page right
In the exam: write N or S above each person who is given a direction, and convert every left/right clue about them

A clue like "A sits to the immediate left of B" has no ambiguity when both face north. When the set mixes directions, convert first and only then place. Sets in this drill all face north, so left and right are as you see them.

3. Clue strength: use the strongest first

Each clue narrows where people can go. The more it narrows, the earlier you use it.

RankClue typeWhy
1Fixed seat (ends, third from end, middle)One seat. Place and tick off at once.
2Exact gap ("k between", "second to the right")Pair spans k + 2 seats, so few placements.
3Adjacent ("immediately")A block of two. Blocks chain: [A B] and [B C] make [A B C].
4One-sided order ("somewhere to the right")Cuts positions but not exactly.
5Negatives (not at an end, not neighbours)Only eliminates. Use last, to finish a branch.

Rule: place rank-1 clues, then use rank-2 and rank-3 clues to extend what is placed, then branch if you must. Negatives come last because they only cross out options.

4. Counting where a pattern can sit

A pattern that spans m consecutive seats can start in n − m + 1 places. "Exactly k between A and B" spans k + 2 seats, so the pair has n − k − 1 placements, doubled because A and B can be either way round.

Pair with k between, n seats:
placements = (n − k − 1) × 2
Example: n = 7, k = 3 spans 5 seats → 3 positions, 6 placements

The symmetry check. When a clue gives a choice (an end-seat clue, or a pair that can go either way), test whether both choices lead to the same seat for someone. Example: seven seats, T at an end, exactly two persons between T and U. If T is seat 1, U is seat 4. If T is seat 7, U is seat 4 again. U is placed without knowing which end T took. This is common in odd-length rows with a gap of 2, so test for it every time.

Long gaps. A pair with 4 or 5 between them can only sit in a few places (for n = 7 and 4 between, only (1,6) and (2,7)). Write those placements out in full: it is quicker than reasoning about them.

5. Blocks and cases

Blocks. "A is immediately right of B" glues [B A]. Treat the block as one object and find where two consecutive seats are free. If another clue says "C immediately right of A", the block becomes [B A C]. Check the open seats: a block of three needs three free neighbouring seats.

Cases. When a clue gives two options ("at one of the ends", a pair that can go either way), write Case 1 and Case 2 on separate lines and carry the rest of the clues through both. A case dies when a clue cannot be satisfied. Two outcomes:

  • One case survives: the arrangement is fixed.
  • Two or more survive: the arrangement is open, and the question will ask what is common to all surviving cases (must be true), whether something is possible in at least one (could be true), or how many cases survive.

A case is killed by a direct contradiction that you can point to: a seat already taken, a block with no room, a negative clue broken. Write the reason in two words next to the case.

"If" questions add a temporary condition. Apply it to the base cases and discard those it contradicts. Do not write the condition into your base grid, because the next question starts from the base again.

7. Traps

  • "Between" excludes both persons. Seats 2 and 6 have 3 people between them, not 4.
  • Third from the left counts the person as 1: it is seat 3.
  • "To the right of" is not "immediately to the right". Read for the word immediately.
  • Neighbour means immediately next, on either side.
  • "At one of the ends" is two cases. Do not pick one because it feels likely.
  • The condition in an "if" question must not leak into the next question.
  • A "must" answer needs every case. One counter-case is enough to reject an option.

8. Worked example 1: a clean set

Six persons A to F in seats 1 to 6, all facing north.

  1. F sits at the extreme right end.
  2. B sits second to the left of F.
  3. D sits immediately to the right of A.
  4. C sits somewhere to the left of A.

Step 1: F = 6. Step 2: B = 4 (second to the left of 6). Seats 1, 2, 3, 5 remain for A, C, D, E.

Step 3: [A D] needs two neighbouring seats among 1, 2, 3, 5. The options are (1,2) and (2,3); seat 5 has no free neighbour on its right (6 is F) and on its left (4 is B).

Step 4: C must be to the left of A. If [A D] were (1,2), no seat is left of A. So [A D] = (2,3), C = 1, and E fills seat 5.

Result: C A D B E F. Check: four clues used, one arrangement left. A negative clue was never needed.

9. Worked example 2: cases

Seven persons P, Q, R, S, T, U, V in seats 1 to 7, all facing north.

  1. T sits at one of the two ends.
  2. Exactly two persons sit between T and U.
  3. V sits third from the left end.
  4. Q sits immediately to the left of V.
  5. R sits immediately to the right of S.
  6. P sits somewhere to the right of U.
  7. R does not sit at either end.

Fixed seats first. V = 3 (clue 3), so Q = 2 (clue 4).

Gap clue. T is at seat 1 or 7 (clue 1) and U is three seats away (clue 2): T = 1 gives U = 4, T = 7 gives U = 4. U = 4 in both cases, so it is placed.

Case 1: T = 1. Seats 5, 6, 7 remain for P, R, S. [S R] needs two neighbours: (5,6) with P = 7, or (6,7) with P = 5. P is right of U (seat 4) in both, so both survive for now.

Case 2: T = 7. Seats 1, 5, 6 remain. [S R] must be (5,6), leaving P = 1, which is left of U. Clue 6 is broken, so Case 2 dies.

Clue 7: R is not at an end. In the sub-case [S R] = (6,7), R would be seat 7: dead. So [S R] = (5,6), P = 7.

Result: T Q V U S R P. The negative clue (7) did the last cut.

If clue 7 were missing, two arrangements would survive (T Q V U S R P and T Q V U P S R), and a question would ask what is common to both: for example "R is not at seat 4" or "T is at seat 1".

The four-minute set protocol

  1. 0:00 to 1:00: draw the seats, list the people, convert the clues to shorthand, glance at the questions.
  2. 1:00 to 2:30: place fixed seats, extend with gaps and blocks, branch at most twice.
  3. 2:30 to 4:00: answer the easy questions first (direct reads), then the hypothetical ones. Do not redraw for each question: use a copy of the grid.
  4. 4:00: if the set will not crack, take the questions that do not depend on the unknown part and skip the rest. A single seat you are sure of can still answer a question.

Under the clock in Drill 4

  1. Each set opens with a 2:30 reading clock. Draw and place clues on paper while it runs. Press Start questions early if you finish sooner.
  2. Then 60 seconds a question, with the clues still on screen.
  3. Sets C and D have more than one arrangement. For "must be true", test each option against both arrangements; for "if" questions, use the condition to pick one.
  4. Marking is +3 / -1. Cut an option by direction check before you guess.
